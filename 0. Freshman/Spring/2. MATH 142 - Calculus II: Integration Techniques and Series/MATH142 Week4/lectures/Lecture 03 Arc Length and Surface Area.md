## 1. The Length of a Curve

**Slice, approximate, sum, limit** — a fourth time.

Cut the curve $y=f(x)$, $a\le x\le b$, into $n$ pieces. Replace each little piece of curve by the **straight chord** joining its endpoints. By Pythagoras, a chord spanning $\Delta x$ horizontally and $\Delta y$ vertically has length

$$\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2} = \sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\right)^2}\;\Delta x$$

As the pieces shrink, $\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\to f'(x)$, and the sum becomes an integral:

$$\boxed{L = \int_a^b\sqrt{1+\big(f'(x)\big)^2}\,dx}$$

**The polygon converges to the curve.** *(Lab 4 measures how fast — the answer is $O(1/n^2)$, and the polygon always **under**estimates, because a chord is shorter than the arc it spans.)*

### In terms of $y$

If the curve is given as $x=g(y)$, slice horizontally instead:

$$L = \int_c^d\sqrt{1+\big(g'(y)\big)^2}\,dy$$

**Use whichever avoids an inverse** — the same rule as yesterday.

## 2. The Trouble With That Square Root

$$\sqrt{1+\big(f'(x)\big)^2}$$

**This is almost never elementary.** Even for very simple $f$, squaring the derivative and adding 1 produces something under a root that no technique from Weeks 0–2 can handle.

### The three outcomes

| $f$ | $\sqrt{1+(f')^2}$ | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| $x^{3/2}$ | $\sqrt{1+\tfrac94x}$ | **easy** — a linear substitution |
| $x^2$ | $\sqrt{1+4x^2}$ | **doable** — trigonometric substitution |
| $\sin x$ | $\sqrt{1+\cos^2x}$ | **impossible** in closed form |

### Example 1 — the easy case

$y = x^{3/2}$ on $[0,1]$: $f' = \tfrac32x^{1/2}$, so $(f')^2 = \tfrac94x$.

$$L = \int_0^1\sqrt{1+\tfrac94 x}\,dx = \left[\frac{8}{27}\left(1+\frac94x\right)^{3/2}\right]_0^1 = \frac{8}{27}\left(\frac{13}{4}\right)^{3/2}-\frac8{27}$$

$$= \boxed{\frac{13\sqrt{13}-8}{27}} \approx 1.4397$$

*Verified symbolically.*

**Curves of the form $y=x^{3/2}$ are in every textbook for exactly this reason** — the $\tfrac32$ is chosen so that $(f')^2$ is linear. It is not a natural curve; it is a curve engineered to be integrable.

### Example 2 — the doable case

$y=x^2$ on $[0,1]$: $L = \displaystyle\int_0^1\sqrt{1+4x^2}\,dx$.

This is a **trigonometric substitution** ($2x = \tan\theta$, Week 2's second pattern), and it produces a $\int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta$ — the awkward integral from Week 1. The result:

$$L = \frac{\sqrt5}{2}+\frac{\operatorname{arcsinh}2}{4} \approx \boxed{1.4789}$$

*Verified symbolically.*

**The arc length of a parabola requires two of the harder techniques in this course.** That is a fair signal of how quickly this material becomes intractable.

*(Equivalently $\tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}+\tfrac14\ln(2+\sqrt5)$, since $\operatorname{arcsinh}u=\ln(u+\sqrt{u^2+1})$. Both forms are correct — Week 2's lesson about unrecognisable-but-equal antiderivatives.)*

### Example 3 — the catenary, which is secretly easy

$y=\cosh x$ on $[0,1]$: $f'=\sinh x$, and the **hyperbolic identity** $1+\sinh^2 x = \cosh^2 x$ gives

$$L = \int_0^1\sqrt{\cosh^2x}\,dx = \int_0^1\cosh x\,dx = \sinh 1 \approx \boxed{1.1752}$$

*Verified: the identity holds, and the integral is $\sinh 1$.*

**The catenary is the shape of a hanging chain**, and this is the one non-trivial curve whose arc length is genuinely clean. The identity $1+\sinh^2=\cosh^2$ is doing exactly the job that $1+\tan^2=\sec^2$ does in trigonometric substitution.

### Example 4 — the impossible case

$y=\sin x$ on $[0,\pi]$:

$$L = \int_0^\pi\sqrt{1+\cos^2x}\,dx \approx 3.8202$$

*Verified numerically: $3.82019778902771$. Symbolically, a CAS returns the integral unevaluated — it has no elementary closed form.*

**This is an elliptic integral.** It is the same object that gives **the circumference of an ellipse** — which is why, despite the ellipse being known since antiquity and its area being simply $\pi ab$, **there is no elementary formula for its perimeter.** Mathematicians spent the 18th century failing to find one; the functions invented in the attempt (elliptic functions) became a major field in their own right.

> **Week 0's opening fact, in geometric form.** You can write down the arc length of essentially any
> curve as an integral. You can evaluate almost none of them.

## 3. Surface Area of Revolution

Rotate the curve $y=f(x)$, $a\le x\le b$, about the $x$-axis. **Slice, approximate, sum, limit** once more — but the approximating piece is now a **frustum** (a cone with its top cut off), not a cylinder.

A band generated by a small arc of length $ds$ at height $f(x)$ has area approximately

$$(\text{circumference})\times(\text{slant width}) = 2\pi f(x)\,ds$$

and $ds = \sqrt{1+(f')^2}\,dx$ is the arc length element from §1. Hence

$$\boxed{S = 2\pi\int_a^b f(x)\sqrt{1+\big(f'(x)\big)^2}\,dx}$$

> **The arc length element, not $dx$.** Using $2\pi f(x)\,dx$ — approximating each band by a
> *cylinder* rather than a frustum — is wrong, and it is the standard error. The slant matters: a
> steeply sloping band has much more surface than its horizontal extent suggests.

### About the $y$-axis

$$S = 2\pi\int_a^b x\sqrt{1+\big(f'(x)\big)^2}\,dx$$

**Again: radius × arc length element.** The radius is the distance to the axis, exactly as in the volume formulas.

### Example 5 — the surface area of a sphere

Rotate $y=\sqrt{r^2-x^2}$ on $[-r,r]$ about the $x$-axis. Here $f' = \dfrac{-x}{\sqrt{r^2-x^2}}$, so

$$1+(f')^2 = 1+\frac{x^2}{r^2-x^2} = \frac{r^2}{r^2-x^2} \implies \sqrt{1+(f')^2} = \frac{r}{\sqrt{r^2-x^2}}$$

and the integrand collapses beautifully:

$$S = 2\pi\int_{-r}^{r}\sqrt{r^2-x^2}\cdot\frac{r}{\sqrt{r^2-x^2}}\,dx = 2\pi r\int_{-r}^r dx = \boxed{4\pi r^2}$$

*Verified symbolically.*

**The two square roots cancel exactly**, leaving a constant integrand. This is **Archimedes' theorem**: the surface area of a sphere equals that of the cylinder circumscribing it, and equally, *equal slices of a sphere by parallel planes have equal surface area* — a fact used in map projections.

### Example 6

Rotate $y=\sqrt x$ on $[0,4]$ about the $x$-axis. Here $f'=\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}$:

$$S = 2\pi\int_0^4\sqrt x\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{4x}}\,dx = 2\pi\int_0^4\sqrt{x+\tfrac14}\,dx = \frac{\pi\big(17\sqrt{17}-1\big)}{6} \approx \boxed{36.177}$$

*Verified symbolically.*

**The simplification $\sqrt x\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{4x}} = \sqrt{x+\frac14}$ is the whole problem.** Combine the roots before integrating; expanding first produces an unpleasant integral.

## 4. What To Take From This Lecture

1. **Arc length: $L=\int\sqrt{1+(f')^2}\,dx$** — Pythagoras on a chord, in the limit.
2. **Surface area: $S=2\pi\int(\text{radius})\,ds$** — with $ds$ the arc length element, **not $dx$**.
3. **These integrals are usually not elementary.** Textbook curves are chosen so that they are.
4. **The arc length of a parabola needs a trigonometric substitution; the arc length of a sine curve cannot be done at all.**
5. **The sphere's surface area falls out because two square roots cancel** — a rare gift.
6. **Combine radicals before integrating.**

## Looking Ahead

Tomorrow's lab pushes both formulas to infinity. Rotating $y=\tfrac1x$ for $x\ge1$ gives a solid with

$$V = \pi\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2} = \pi \qquad\text{but}\qquad S = 2\pi\int_1^\infty\frac1x\sqrt{1+\frac1{x^4}}\,dx = \infty$$

*(both verified)*

**Finite volume, infinite surface area.** The volume converges by Week 3's $p$-test with $p=2$; the surface diverges by comparison with $p=1$. **This week's geometry and last week's analysis, in one object.**
