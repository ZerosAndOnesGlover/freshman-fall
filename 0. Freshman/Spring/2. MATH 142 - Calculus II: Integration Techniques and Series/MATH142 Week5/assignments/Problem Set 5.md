## Part A — Parametric Curves (5 pts each)

**A1.** Eliminate the parameter and identify the curve: $x = 3\cos t$, $y = 2\sin t$, $t\in[0,2\pi]$.

*State the orientation and the starting point.*

**A2.** Eliminate the parameter: $x = t^2-2t$, $y = t+1$.

*Here it is easier to solve for $t$ from the $y$ equation.*

**A3.** Eliminate the parameter: $x = e^t$, $y = e^{2t}$, $t\in\mathbb{R}$.

*Be careful to state the resulting curve's **range** — the Cartesian equation describes more than the parametrisation traces.*

**A4.** The curve $x = t^3-3t$, $y = t^2-1$ **crosses itself.**

Find the parameter values at which this happens and the point of crossing. *(Hint: two values $t_1\neq t_2$ must give the same $x$ **and** the same $y$. Start with $y$.)*

## Part B — Calculus with Parametric Curves (6 pts each)

**B1.** For $x=t^2$, $y=t^3-3t$, find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ at $t=2$.

**B2.** For $x=t^2-t$, $y=t^3-3t$, find all parameter values giving **horizontal** tangents and all giving **vertical** tangents.

**B3.** Find the arc length of $x=t^2$, $y=t^3$ for $t\in[0,1]$.

*Compare your answer with the arc length of $y=x^{3/2}$ on $[0,1]$ from Week 4. Explain the relationship.*

**B4.** Find the area under one arch of the cycloid $x = t-\sin t$, $y=1-\cos t$, $t\in[0,2\pi]$.

**B5.** The astroid is $x=a\cos^3t$, $y=a\sin^3t$. Rotate its **first-quadrant arc** ($t\in\left[0,\tfrac\pi2\right]$) about the $x$-axis and find the surface area.

*You computed the speed for this curve in Lecture 2, Example 6.*

## Part C — Polar Coordinates (6 pts each)

**C1.** Find the area enclosed by the cardioid $r = 1-\sin\theta$.

**C2.** Find the area of **one petal** of the rose $r = 2\sin3\theta$.

*State the range of $\theta$ tracing exactly one petal, and justify it by finding where $r=0$.*

**C3.** Find the arc length of the Archimedean spiral $r=\theta$ for $\theta\in[0,\pi]$.

*This needs a trigonometric substitution from Week 2.*

**C4.** Sketch $r = 2a\cos\theta$ and find the range of $\theta$ that traces it **exactly once**.

Then compute its enclosed area two ways — over your range, and over $[0,2\pi]$ — and explain the discrepancy.

**C5.** Convert $r = 4\sin\theta$ to Cartesian form and identify the curve.

## Part D — Concept (10 pts each)

**D1.** *(Where the $\tfrac12$ comes from.)*

- (a) Derive $A = \tfrac12\int_\alpha^\beta r^2\,d\theta$ from the slice–approximate–sum–limit pattern. State explicitly what shape each slice is approximated by, and where the $\tfrac12$ enters.
- (b) A student proposes instead to compute polar area as $\int y\,dx$ with $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$. Explain why this is **not** simply the same formula rewritten — what is geometrically different about the two families of slices?
- (c) Use your formula to find the area of the disc $r\le a$, and confirm it gives $\pi a^2$.

**D2.** *(Which curves have closed forms.)*

- (a) Compute the arc length of one arch of the cycloid $x=a(t-\sin t)$, $y=a(1-\cos t)$, showing the step where the square root disappears.
- (b) Write down the integral for the perimeter of the ellipse $x=a\cos t$, $y=b\sin t$ with $a\neq b$. **Do not attempt to evaluate it.**
- (c) One of these has an elementary closed form and the other provably does not. **Identify precisely what is different about the two integrands** that causes this.
- (d) The cycloid cannot even be written as $y=f(x)$, while the ellipse is a conic section known since antiquity. Comment, in two or three sentences, on what this says about predicting which problems are tractable.
