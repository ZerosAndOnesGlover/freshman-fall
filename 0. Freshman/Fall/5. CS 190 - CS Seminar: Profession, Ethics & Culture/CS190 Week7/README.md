### Copyright, Patents, Trade Secrets, and the DMCA

### Why This Week Exists

Software fits badly into every existing category of intellectual property — it is simultaneously an expressive work, a functional machine, and a trade secret. The resulting doctrinal mess is not an accident, and it directly governs what you will be permitted to write, publish, and reuse.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of Week 7, you should be able to:

1. Explain why software resists clean classification under copyright, patent, and trade-secret law.
2. State the idea/expression dichotomy and the four-factor fair use test, and apply them to code.
3. Summarise what Google v. Oracle settled and what it left open.
4. Explain the DMCA's anti-circumvention provision (§1201) and its friction with the right to repair.
5. Explain why copyright's automatic attachment is what makes open source licensing possible at all — a licensor can only grant what they already hold.

### A Note on Open Source Licences

The curriculum lists "Open Source Licenses" under this week. **The licence taxonomy itself is taught in Week 2** (§5.4), alongside the open source ecosystem it belongs to: permissive versus copyleft, MIT/BSD/Apache 2.0 against GPL/LGPL/AGPL, licence compatibility, and contributor licence agreements.

That placement is deliberate and the coverage is not reduced by it. **Week 2 explains what the licences say; Week 7 explains why they bind.** A licence is an exercise of the copyright the author automatically holds, so the mechanics only make sense once the underlying right does — which is why §6 of this week's lecture returns to copyleft violation and classifies it, correctly, as copyright infringement rather than a breach of etiquette.

If you want the licence table, it is in [[Lecture Week 2]] §5.4.
