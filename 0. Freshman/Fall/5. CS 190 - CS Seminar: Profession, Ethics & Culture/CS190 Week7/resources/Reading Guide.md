## Required Reading

### 1. *Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc.*, 593 U.S. \_\_\_ (2021) — Majority Opinion (Breyer, J.)
**~20 pages of the majority opinion. Free at supremecourt.gov or oyez.org (oyez.org also has excellent case background and oral argument audio).**

Read the majority opinion directly. Pay particular attention to how the Court frames the API declaring code's function (Part II) and the application of the four fair use factors (Part III) — this is the most important single case for understanding how copyright law is actually applied to software specifics, rather than general principles.

**Guiding questions:**
- The Court assumed, without deciding, that the API declaring code was copyrightable, and ruled only on fair use. Why might the Court have preferred this narrower path to resolving the case, compared to ruling directly on copyrightability?
- Justice Thomas's dissent (optional but recommended) argues the majority's fair use analysis was flawed and that Google's copying was commercially motivated wholesale copying, not transformative use. Construct the strongest version of this dissenting argument and evaluate it against the majority's reasoning.

### 2. *Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank International*, 573 U.S. 208 (2014) — Majority Opinion (Thomas, J.)
**~15 pages. Free at supremecourt.gov or oyez.org.**

Read for the two-step framework itself (Part III of the opinion) — you do not need deep familiarity with the underlying settlement-risk-mitigation patent claims, just the Court's reasoning about why "implement an abstract idea on a generic computer" fails to clear the patent-eligibility bar.

**Guiding question:**
- The opinion says using a computer to implement a well-known practice does not, by itself, supply the "inventive concept" needed for patent eligibility. Can you construct a hypothetical software patent claim that you believe *would* pass the Alice test — i.e., a genuine technical improvement to computer functionality itself, rather than an abstract idea merely computerized? Defend why your example clears the bar where the Alice patent did not.

### 3. Samuelson, P. — "Freedom to Tinker" (*Theoretical Inquiries in Law*, 2016) — excerpt on DMCA § 1201 and security research
**~15 pages (read the sections addressing § 1201's effect on security research specifically). Free PDF via SSRN or the author's academic page.**

Pamela Samuelson is one of the most cited legal scholars on software IP law. This piece addresses the tension between DMCA anti-circumvention and legitimate technical activities (security research, interoperability, accessibility) directly.

**Guiding question:**
- Samuelson argues the triennial exemption rulemaking process is an inadequate substitute for a durable legal safe harbor. What would a better structural solution look like, and what are the tradeoffs of building more permanent exceptions into § 1201 versus keeping the current renewable-exemption model?

## Optional / Enrichment Reading

### 4. Lemley, M. & Casey, B. — "Fair Learning" (*Texas Law Review*, 2021)
**Free PDF via SSRN.** One of the earliest rigorous legal analyses of whether training machine learning models on copyrighted data constitutes fair use — written before the current wave of generative AI litigation but establishing much of the analytical framework courts are now applying.

### 5. Electronic Frontier Foundation — "Unsafe at Any Bit: DMCA's Chilling Effects on Security Research" (ongoing EFF resource page)
**Free at eff.org.** A running, practitioner-oriented account of specific documented cases where security researchers faced DMCA-related legal threats or chilling effects, complementing Samuelson's more academic treatment.
