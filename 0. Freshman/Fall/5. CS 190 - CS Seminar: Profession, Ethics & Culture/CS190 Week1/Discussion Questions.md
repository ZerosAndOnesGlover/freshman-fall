# Discussion Questions — Week 1 Seminar

---

## Part 1: History as Argument (~10 min)

1. The lecture presents computing history as a series of *ideas responding to other ideas* — Turing responding to Hilbert, the transistor responding to vacuum tube unreliability, the compiler responding to machine-code tedium. Can you identify a place in the history where you think the "response" framing breaks down — where something was invented for reasons that weren't about solving a prior problem?

2. Abbate's reading challenges the "ARPANET was designed to survive nuclear attack" narrative. Does correcting a historical myth change anything practically, or is it just academic housekeeping? Make the case both ways.

## Part 2: The Turing Test and Its Legacy (~15 min)

3. Turing lists the "Argument from Consciousness" as an objection: a machine can only simulate intelligent behavior, not genuinely think or feel, so it's not truly intelligent. Turing's response is essentially: we can't verify consciousness in other humans either — we only infer it. Do you find this response sufficient? What does it miss, if anything?

4. As of your enrollment in this program, large language models produce text that many users find indistinguishable from human writing in ordinary conversation. Two positions: (a) This vindicates Turing's prediction and shows machines can think. (b) LLMs are sophisticated pattern-matchers that simulate intelligence without instantiating it — a very different thing. Argue for one of these positions, then identify the strongest objection to it.

5. Ada Lovelace wrote in 1843 that the Analytical Engine "has no power of originating anything. It can only do what we *know* how to order it to perform." Is this claim still true of modern neural networks? If it is, what does that mean for how we should talk about AI "creativity"? If it isn't, what changed?

## Part 3: Credit, Culture, and Consequences (~15 min)

6. Grace Hopper's compiler was initially resisted because engineers doubted a machine could translate code as well as a human. This is a recurring pattern: new abstractions are resisted on efficiency grounds, then adopted when efficiency concerns prove overblown. Can you identify a current version of this argument — something in computing today that is being resisted on "efficiency" grounds that might look, in 20 years, the way Hopper's critics look now?

7. The lecture describes Silicon Valley culture's "move fast and break things" philosophy. The things that get "broken" are not always abstract — they include people's privacy, livelihoods, democratic processes, and mental health outcomes. Is "move fast and break things" a defensible engineering philosophy under any framing, or is it simply a rationalization for not thinking about consequences? Be specific.

8. The lecture notes that Turing did not foresee the internet, and Berners-Lee did not foresee social media. Does the fact that designers routinely fail to foresee second-order consequences of what they build mean that trying to foresee consequences is futile — or does it mean we need to be more systematic about it? What would "being more systematic" even look like in practice?

## Closing

9. Pick one figure from the Week 1 timeline and make the case that they are *underrated* in the standard history — that their contribution is more significant than popular accounts suggest. Conversely, pick one who you think is *overrated*. Justify both.
