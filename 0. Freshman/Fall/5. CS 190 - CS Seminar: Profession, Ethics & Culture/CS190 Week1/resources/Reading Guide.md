# Reading Guide — Week 1

---

## Required Reading

### 1. Turing, A. — "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (Mind, 1950)
**~20 pages. Free widely online (search title + "Mind 1950").**

This is the paper that introduced the Turing Test — though Turing never called it that. It is also a remarkably broad philosophical essay covering objections to machine intelligence ranging from theology to mathematical logic. It is not a technical paper; it is an argument, and one of the most carefully constructed in computing history.

**Guiding questions:**
- Turing lists nine objections to the idea of machine intelligence and responds to each. Which response do you find most convincing? Which do you find weakest?
- Turing's "imitation game" involves a machine pretending to be human. Is passing the test a sufficient condition for intelligence? A necessary one? Neither?
- In 2024 (your present), large language models can pass some versions of the Turing Test. Does this settle the question Turing was asking, or does the question remain open? Why?

### 2. Abbate, J. — *Inventing the Internet*, Chapter 1 ("The Origins of the Internet")
**~30 pages. Available in university library.**

A historian's account of ARPANET's origins — importantly, one that is skeptical of the popular mythology. Abbate argues that the "designed to survive nuclear attack" story is largely retrospective, and the actual design priorities were different.

**Guiding question:**
- If the "designed for nuclear resilience" origin story is mythologized, does that change how we should think about the Internet's design principles? Why or why not?

### 3. Isaacson, W. — *The Innovators*, Chapter 1 excerpt: "Ada Lovelace"
**~25 pages. University library or excerpts circulated by course.**

Isaacson writes popular technology history with a focus on who actually deserves credit. The Lovelace chapter is useful here both for the historical content (what she actually contributed vs. what is often claimed) and as a case study in the history of who gets credited in technology — a question that remains live in open source, AI research, and industry today.

**Guiding question:**
- Isaacson is careful to separate "what Lovelace actually wrote" from "what she is often claimed to have written." After reading, where do you land on her historical significance? Does the question of exact credit matter, or is the broader narrative contribution enough?

## Optional / Enrichment Reading

### 4. Ceruzzi, P. — *A History of Modern Computing*, Introduction
A standard academic history of computing — drier than Isaacson but more rigorous. Useful if you want the chronology in more depth.

### 5. Levy, S. — *Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution*, Prologue and Chapter 1
**Free excerpts widely available.**

Levy's 1984 account of the hacker culture at MIT in the late 1950s and 1960s — the cultural origin of much of what Silicon Valley would later become. Read for the cultural texture, not the technical accuracy.
