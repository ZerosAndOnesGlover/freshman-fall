# CS 190 · Week 12
## Course Reflection

**Due:** Friday 18 December 2026, 17:00 (end of Week 12). **The final deliverable of the course.**
**Length:** 600–800 words.
**Weight:** Participation component (40% of the course grade).

---

## What This Is Not

Not a course evaluation. That is separate, anonymous, and does not go to the person marking this.

Not a summary of what was covered. The syllabus already records that.

Not an exercise in saying the course was valuable. **A reflection concluding that your views did not
change, defended properly, scores as well as one describing a conversion** — and is considerably rarer
and more interesting.

---

## What This Is

An account of **what changed and what did not**, with reasons.

Ethical reasoning is not a body of content to be retained; it is a practice that either survives
contact with a career or does not. This assignment asks you to state, while it is fresh, what you
actually now think — so that you have something specific to be held to, including by yourself, in
four years' time.

---

## Required Sections

### 1. One position you changed (≈200 words)

Name a view you held in Week 0 and no longer hold, or hold differently.

State **what you thought**, **what you think now**, and — this is the part that carries the marks —
**what did it**. A reading, an argument in seminar, a classmate's presentation, a case that would not
fit your framework. Be specific: "the course made me think more carefully" is not an answer.

If a **classmate's** argument changed your mind, say whose. Week 11's feedback forms asked you to
record exactly this, which is why you were asked to keep your copies.

### 2. One position you held onto, under pressure (≈200 words)

Name a view that was directly challenged and that you retained.

State the **strongest objection** you encountered and why it did not move you. Not the weakest — the
one that gave you the most trouble.

**Holding a position under pressure and being stubborn look identical from the inside.** The
difference is whether you can state the objection fairly, and this section is where you demonstrate
you can.

### 3. The question you cannot answer (≈150 words)

Name a question the course raised that you cannot resolve.

Say what makes it hard: whether the difficulty is empirical (you do not know a fact), conceptual (you
do not know what would settle it), or practical (you know what is right and do not know how it could
be done from a first job).

**These three are different problems with different remedies**, and telling them apart is most of the
value of this section.

### 4. What you commit to (≈150 words)

One thing you will do differently, specific enough to fail at.

**Not:** "I will consider the ethical implications of my work."
**Yes:** "Before I ship a model that scores people, I will look at its error rates broken out by
group, and I will write down what rate I would consider unacceptable **before** I look at the
numbers."

State how you would know, in a year, whether you had done it.

## Also Include

**Your Week 11 audience record.** One or two lines: how many questions you asked across the three
presentation sessions, and which presentation changed your thinking most, and how.

---

## How This Is Marked

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Specificity** | Named views, named arguments, named sources. Generalities score poorly however well written |
| **Honesty about difficulty** | Sections 2 and 3 reward stating the objection you could not fully answer. Papers in which every question resolves neatly are marked down |
| **Self-knowledge** | Whether you can distinguish having changed your mind from having learned what one is supposed to say |

You are **not** marked on the content of your positions. A reflection defending a view the instructor
disagrees with, argued honestly, scores full marks. This has been the standard since Week 3 and it
does not change for the last assignment.

---

## The Retrospective Session

The final 10 minutes of the Week 12 seminar, after the guest has left, are a spoken retrospective —
the last conversation the section has as a group.

Three questions, around the room:

1. **What did this course get wrong?** Which week was thin, which reading did not earn its place,
   which argument was presented as settled that is not. This is genuinely used to revise the course.
2. **What did you expect to be a bigger deal than it turned out to be?**
3. **What are you now worried about that you were not worried about in September?**

The third question is the one the course exists for. **It is a good outcome, not a bad one.** You are
about to spend a career building things that affect people who never agreed to be affected. Noticing
that is the beginning of being able to do it well.
