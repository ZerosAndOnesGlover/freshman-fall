# CS 190 · CS Seminar: Profession, Ethics & Culture
## Week 12: Guest Speaker — CS Professional; Course Reflection

**Format:** Weekly 1-hour seminar — guest remarks and questions, then a closing retrospective.
**Assessment for this course (overall):** Participation 40%, Position Papers 60%
**This week's deliverables:** Question preparation (before the seminar) and the **Course Reflection**,
due end of Week 12 — the final deliverable of the course.

---

### Why This Week Exists

Eleven weeks of this course have been conducted at one remove. You have read about decisions other
people made, in cases that reached publication because they went badly enough to be written up. That
is a systematically distorted sample, and the distortion has been the course's main unavoidable
weakness.

**The guest is the correction.** One practitioner, describing what the work consists of and a
decision they are still not sure about. They are one data point, not an authority on the field — but
they are a data point of a kind the readings cannot supply.

The reflection is the other half. Ethical reasoning is not content to be retained; it is a practice
that either survives contact with a career or does not. **The reflection asks you to write down what
you actually now think, specifically enough to be held to it later** — including by yourself.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of Week 12, you should be able to:

1. Prepare a question that only a practitioner could answer, and distinguish it from one answerable from a biography or a search engine.
2. Weigh first-hand professional testimony appropriately — taking accounts of what happened seriously while treating generalisations from a single career with care.
3. Identify a position you changed and name **what** changed it.
4. State the strongest objection to a position you retained, and say why it did not move you.
5. Distinguish an unresolved question that is empirical, one that is conceptual, and one that is practical — three different problems with three different remedies.
6. State a professional commitment specific enough that you could fail at it and know that you had.

### On the Reflection

It is not a course evaluation — that is separate and anonymous — and it is not an exercise in saying
the course was valuable. **A reflection concluding that your views did not change, defended properly,
scores as well as one describing a conversion**, and is rarer and more interesting.

You are not marked on the content of your positions. You are marked on specificity, on honesty about
what you could not answer, and on whether you can tell having changed your mind from having learned
what one is supposed to say. That standard has held since Week 3 and does not change for the last
assignment.

Keep your Week 11 peer feedback forms. Question 6 on each asked whether the presentation changed your
mind, and the reflection asks you to report exactly that.

### The Retrospective

The last ten minutes, after the guest leaves. Three questions around the room, of which the third is
the one the course exists for:

> **What are you now worried about that you were not worried about in September?**

**It is a good outcome, not a bad one.** You are about to spend a career building things that affect
people who never agreed to be affected. Noticing that is the beginning of being able to do it well.

### Connections

**Back:** everything. `Glossary Week 12.md` is a course-wide review rather than a set of new terms,
and states the twelve recurring concepts in the form the course finally arrived at — most of them
correctives to a comfortable idea rather than the idea itself.

**Forward:** CS 190 has no direct successor course, which is exactly the problem it is designed
around. The professional obligations discussed here are not revisited in a later ethics module; they
recur in CS 201, CS 202, and every project course after them, as constraints on work that will be
assessed on whether it functions. **Whether the reasoning survives is now your responsibility rather
than the timetable's.**
