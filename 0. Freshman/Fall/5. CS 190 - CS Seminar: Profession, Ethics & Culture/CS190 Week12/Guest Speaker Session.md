# CS 190 · Week 12
## Guest Speaker Session: A CS Professional

**Format:** 60-minute seminar. Roughly 20 minutes of remarks, 40 minutes of questions.
**Assessment:** Participation. **Preparation is required** — see `Question Preparation.md`.

---

## 1. Who Comes, and Why It Varies

The Week 12 speaker is a working computer scientist or engineer **five to twenty-five years into a
career**, invited each term from a different part of the field. Over past terms the visitor has been
a backend engineer at a large consumer platform, a security researcher, a machine learning
practitioner at a health-care startup, an engineering manager, a public-sector technologist, and an
academic who left industry.

The speaker is announced on the course page in Week 10, with a short biography and any reading they
ask you to do.

**The variation is deliberate.** The course has been teaching that "a career in computing" is not one
thing, and a single recurring speaker would quietly contradict that. It also means the visitor is
**not an authority on the field as a whole**. They are one informed data point, which is exactly how
you should treat what they say.

---

## 2. What the Speaker Is Asked to Do

Each visitor is asked, in advance, to address four things in their opening remarks:

1. **What their work actually consists of** — hours, tasks, the ratio of code to everything else.
   Specifically not a description of their employer's products.
2. **A decision they got wrong**, technical or ethical, and what it cost.
3. **A time they raised a concern**, and what happened — including the case where nothing happened.
4. **What they wish they had known at the point you are at now**, stated concretely enough to act on.

Item 2 is the one guests find hardest and the one students report as most valuable. **Ten weeks of
this course have discussed decisions in the abstract**; the point of the visit is to hear someone
describe one they are still not sure about.

Speakers are told explicitly that this is an ethics seminar, that students have read the ACM Code and
studied algorithmic bias, privacy, IP, disclosure, industry culture and automation, and that they
should expect **substantive questions rather than career-advice questions.**

---

## 3. Your Obligations

**Come with two prepared questions.** Written down, submitted beforehand via
`Question Preparation.md`. This is participation-graded.

Prepared questions exist for a specific reason. Q&A sessions default to a handful of confident
students asking what it takes to get hired. That is a legitimate question and it is not what forty
minutes with a practitioner is for.

**Ask at least one of yours.** If time runs out before you are called, submit it with your reflection
and note that it went unasked — this costs nothing.

---

## 4. How to Treat What You Hear

The visitor knows things you do not, and they are also **one person with one career, generalising
from a sample of one.** Both halves matter.

Take seriously:
- Descriptions of **what happened**. First-hand accounts of decisions, incidents and consequences are
  the reason to have a guest at all.
- Descriptions of **what a job consists of day to day**, which is systematically misrepresented in
  every other source available to you.

Treat with care:
- **"That never happens in practice."** It may not happen at their employer, in their specialty, at
  their seniority. Ask what their vantage point is rather than treating the claim as settled.
- **Career advice generalised from one path.** Survivorship is severe here: you are hearing from
  someone whose path worked out well enough to be invited to speak about it.
- **Claims about the field's ethics that flatter the speaker's own choices.** This is not cynicism.
  It is the same standard the course has applied to every source, and it applies to visitors too.

**Disagreeing with a guest is permitted and welcomed**, provided it is done as this room does it —
about the argument, courteously, and with the recognition that they came voluntarily to be questioned
by a room of first-years, which is a generous thing to do.

---

## 5. Questions Worth Asking

Illustrative, not a list to read from:

- *"You described a decision you got wrong. What would have had to be different for you to have got
  it right — a policy, a person, more time, different information?"*
- *"When you raised that concern, what did it cost you?"*
- *"We read the ACM Code in Week 3. Has it ever been invoked where you work — by anyone, in any
  meeting?"*
- *"What proportion of your week is writing code, and what has that number done over your career?"*
- *"What does your team do about crunch, as opposed to what your company says about it?"*
- *"Is there something the industry believes about itself that you have concluded is false?"*

And a question the course has earned the right to ask:

- *"What are you building right now that you are not certain is a good idea?"*
