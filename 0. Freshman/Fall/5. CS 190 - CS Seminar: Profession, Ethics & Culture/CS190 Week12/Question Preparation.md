# CS 190 · Week 12
## Question Preparation

**Due:** Before the Week 12 seminar. Submit on the course page.
**Length:** Roughly 250 words. Participation-graded.

---

## Why This Is Required

Left to itself, a Q&A with a visiting professional converges on the same four questions: how do I get
hired, what should I learn, is a master's worth it, what is the interview like.

Those are real questions and there are better places to ask them. **Forty minutes with a practitioner
who has agreed to discuss decisions they got wrong is not a recruiting session**, and it is wasted if
nobody comes prepared to use it.

Submitting questions in advance also lets the instructor group them, so that the session covers ground
rather than circling.

---

## What to Submit

### Part A — Two questions (≈100 words)

**Question 1 must be about a decision.** Something the speaker did, decided, or declined — how they
reasoned about it, what it cost, what they would do differently. Not about the industry in general;
about them.

**Question 2 is yours to choose**, subject to the constraints below.

For each, add **one sentence** on why you are asking it — what you expect to learn, or what you think
the answer will reveal.

### Part B — Connect one question to the course (≈100 words)

Take one of your two questions and identify the week it comes from — algorithmic bias, privacy, AI
and society, IP, disclosure, industry culture, automation. State:

- what the course taught you about that topic;
- what the course could **not** tell you, because it is a matter of practice rather than principle;
- how the answer might change your view.

**This is the part that is actually marked.** It shows whether you can tell the difference between a
question about facts and a question about how principles survive contact with a workplace.

### Part C — One thing you expect to hear that you will distrust (≈50 words)

Name in advance a claim you think is likely to come up and that you would want evidence for.

Not a prediction that the speaker will be wrong. A commitment, made **before** you are in the room and
inclined to agree with a friendly person who has come to talk to you, about where you intend to keep
your judgement switched on.

---

## Constraints on Questions

**A good question here:**
- is answerable from the speaker's own experience;
- is short enough to ask in fifteen seconds;
- does not have an obvious correct answer the speaker would be embarrassed to contradict;
- would produce a different answer from different practitioners — which is what makes it worth asking
  a person rather than a search engine.

**Avoid:**
- *"What advice would you give someone starting out?"* — invites platitudes and gets them.
- *"What's it like working at [employer]?"* — recruiting-page territory.
- Anything answerable from the speaker's public biography.
- Questions that require the speaker to criticise a named current employer or colleague. **They have
  a job to go back to on Monday**, and putting a guest in that position is a failure of manners, not
  a display of rigour. Ask about the decision, not about the people.

---

## A Note on Courtesy

The visitor is coming voluntarily, unpaid, to be questioned by a room of first-year students about
the parts of their career they are least sure of. That is a generous thing to do.

Courtesy and rigour are not in tension here. **The most respectful thing you can do with a guest's
time is to ask them something hard that only they can answer.**
