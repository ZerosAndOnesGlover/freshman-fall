# CS 190 · Week 11
## Presentation Brief: Position Paper Topics

**Format:** 60-minute seminar. Every student presents.
**Length:** **5 minutes** presenting, **3 minutes** questions. Timed strictly — see §5.
**Weight:** Participation component. **Presenting is a course requirement**; a student who does not
present cannot pass the participation component, which is 40% of the grade.

---

## 1. What You Are Presenting

**The argument from your Position Paper #3, not the topic of it.**

This distinction is the whole assignment, and it is where most first-year presentations go wrong. The
difference:

| ❌ A topic summary | ✅ An argument |
| --- | --- |
| "I wrote about crunch culture." | "I argue that individuals bear a binding obligation regarding crunch, and that it derives from complicity rather than causation." |
| "Here is some background on burnout." | "Here is the objection that nearly defeated my position, and here is why it does not." |
| Ends with "so it's complicated." | Ends with a claim someone in the room could disagree with. |

You have five minutes. That is enough time for **one claim, one reason, one objection, one response**
— and nothing else. Attempting more produces a presentation in which nothing is actually established.

You may present a position you have changed your mind about since writing the paper. Say so if you
have; that is a strength, not an admission.

---

## 2. Required Structure

You are not required to use these words, but all four elements must be identifiably present.

**1. The claim** *(≈45 seconds).* State your position in one or two sentences, at the start. Do not
build to it. This is not a mystery novel, and an audience that does not know where you are going
cannot follow you there.

**2. The reason** *(≈90 seconds).* Why should anyone believe it? One line of argument, developed
properly. Two lines of argument each half-developed is worse than one done well.

**3. The strongest objection** *(≈90 seconds).* Not a weak objection you can knock over — **the
objection that gave you the most trouble when you were writing.** State it in its strongest form,
as its proponent would state it.

**4. Your response, and what it costs you** *(≈75 seconds).* Answer the objection. If the answer is
partial, say which part remains unanswered. **Conceding something real is worth more marks than
pretending the objection was easy.**

---

## 3. Materials

**Slides are optional and are not marked.** If you use them: **no more than three**, and no slide
should contain a paragraph. A single well-chosen number, quotation, or diagram is worth more than a
bulleted outline of what you are about to say.

**You may not read from a script.** Notes are permitted and encouraged — a card with your claim, your
objection, and your response written out. Reading continuous prose aloud is the single most reliable
way to lose an audience, and it will be marked down under *delivery*.

**No handout is required.** If your argument genuinely turns on a passage of text or a table of
figures, bring copies.

---

## 4. Question Period

Three minutes. Every presentation gets questions; a silent room is a failure of the audience, not a
mercy to the presenter.

**As presenter:**
- "I don't know" is an acceptable answer and is marked neutrally. **Inventing an answer is marked
  down.**
- "That's a good objection and I'd need to think about it" is acceptable **once**.
- If a question misunderstands your claim, restate the claim rather than answering the wrong question.

**As audience:** you are required to ask questions across the session — see `Peer Feedback Form.md`.
A good question is short, is about the argument rather than the topic, and is not a speech with a
question mark at the end.

---

## 5. Timing

Five minutes is short, and you will not discover this until you rehearse. **Rehearse aloud, with a
timer, at least twice.** Reading silently takes about half the time speaking does, and the resulting
misjudgement is the most common cause of a truncated presentation.

Timing is enforced:

- **A card is raised at 4:00** — one minute remaining.
- **At 5:00 you are stopped**, mid-sentence if necessary.

This is not harshness for its own sake. Everyone in the room has the same five minutes, and running
over takes them from the person who follows you. **Finishing at 4:30 with your argument complete is a
better presentation than being cut off at 5:00 mid-objection.**

---

## 6. What Is Being Assessed

See `Presentation Rubric.md` for the marking scheme. In brief, the weight is on **whether an argument
was made**, not on polish. A nervous, halting delivery of a real argument scores well above a fluent
delivery of a topic summary.
