### Why This Week Exists

The course has spent ten weeks on written argument. Written argument is patient: you can revise it,
soften it, and never meet the person who disagrees.

**Spoken argument is not patient.** You get five minutes, you cannot revise, and the strongest
objection is raised by a person sitting three metres away who has to be answered now. That is the
condition under which almost all professional ethical argument actually happens — in a design review,
a postmortem, a meeting about a deadline — and it is a distinct skill from writing.

This week is also where the course's central claim gets tested socially rather than individually.
Twenty-four people will present incompatible positions on questions that matter, in the same room,
and the room has to remain one in which people are willing to say what they think.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of Week 11, you should be able to:

1. Compress an argument to its load-bearing parts — one claim, one reason, one objection, one response — and deliver it in five minutes.
2. State a position early and explicitly rather than building to it.
3. State an opposing view in the form its proponent would accept, and respond without overclaiming.
4. Concede what you cannot answer, out loud, in front of an audience.
5. Reconstruct someone else's argument accurately in your own words.
6. Ask a question that is about the argument rather than the topic, and that is not a speech.

### The One Thing That Determines Your Mark

**Present the argument from your paper, not the topic of it.**

Fourteen of the twenty points are for whether an argument was made: a claim someone could disagree
with, a reason developed properly, and the strongest objection engaged honestly. Three points are for
delivery, and nervousness costs none of them.

The most reliable way to lose marks is to describe a subject for five minutes and end on "so it's
complicated." It is complicated. **That is why you were asked for a position.**

### Ground Rules

The rule established in Week 9 applies in full:

> **Arguments are about evidence and reasoning. No argument may require anyone present to defend
> their own existence in the room as a debating position.**

Some presentations will draw on personal experience of the conditions Week 9 described. Presenters
may be arguing from where they stand. Disagree with the argument — which is what has been offered for
disagreement — and do not name what a presenter's argument implies about them but did not state.
