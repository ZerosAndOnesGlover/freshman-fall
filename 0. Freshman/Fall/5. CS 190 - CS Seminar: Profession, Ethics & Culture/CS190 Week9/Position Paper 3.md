## The Prompt

The two previous papers concerned decisions with identifiable agents. Paper #1 asked what an
individual engineer owes when their employer's instruction conflicts with the ACM Code — a decision
with a decider. Paper #2 asked who is responsible when a correctly-functioning technology causes harm
— contested, but still a question about assigning responsibility to identifiable parties.

**This paper concerns harms with no such structure.**

The decline in women's participation in computing after 1984 was not decided by anyone. No meeting
produced it. Crunch persists across an industry despite being unprofitable, and no individual chose
that. The conditions the WHO describes as producing burnout are not the policy of any particular firm.
These are **emergent outcomes of many uncoordinated decisions**, each of which may have been locally
reasonable.

Such problems generate a characteristic argument, which you have probably already made yourself:
*the problem is structural, I have no power to change the structure, therefore I have no obligation
regarding it.* The argument is not stupid. It is also extremely convenient, and it is available to
everyone simultaneously — which, if it were sound, would mean nobody is obliged to do anything about
harms that everyone is producing.

**Your task:** take and defend a specific position on the following question.

> *What, if anything, does an individual with no institutional power owe in respect of a harm that is
> produced structurally rather than by identifiable decisions — and what makes that obligation binding
> rather than merely admirable?*

---

## Requirements

Your paper must do all of the following:

**1. State a clear position in your opening paragraph.** Not "it's complicated" — a position that
could be disagreed with.

**2. Choose one concrete structural problem and stay with it.** Options include the demographic
composition of the field, the persistence of crunch, the conditions producing burnout, or the
help-seeking barriers discussed in the lecture. **One.** Depth is required; breadth is not.

**3. Take a position on the *bindingness* question, not merely the desirability one.** It is not
enough to argue that an individual *should* act well. You must say what makes the obligation an
obligation — something one can fail to meet — rather than a supererogatory nicety one earns credit
for. If you conclude it is *not* binding, defend that; it is a legitimate position and requires the
same rigour.

**4. Engage with the strongest version of the opposing view.** If you argue for individual obligation,
engage seriously with the futility objection: that individual acts against structural problems are
gestural, that they impose real costs on the individual for negligible aggregate effect, and that
treating them as obligatory unfairly burdens those least able to bear the cost — often the very people
the structure already disadvantages. If you argue against obligation, engage with the objection that
your argument is available to everyone at once, and that a principle everyone can invoke to excuse
themselves cannot be a valid moral principle.

**5. Distinguish the levels at which obligation might operate.** An individual, a team lead, a company,
and a profession are not in the same position. Your account should say something about *why* the level
matters — whether obligation scales with power, and if so, whether it ever reaches zero.

**6. Reach a conclusion with practical content.** What should a person in your position actually do
next year? Your answer should be specific enough that someone could fail to follow it.

## What Makes This Paper Harder Than #2

Paper #2 removed the scaffold of an authoritative code and asked you to build a framework for
assigning responsibility. **This paper removes the identifiable agent.**

Most ethical frameworks you have available — the ACM Code, consequentialism, deontology — were built
to evaluate *actions by agents*. They handle structural harm awkwardly, because there is no action to
evaluate and no agent to hold responsible. Virtue ethics (Week 3) fares somewhat better, since it asks
what kind of person one is rather than what a given act produces, but it correspondingly struggles to
say why anything is *required*.

**You will therefore be arguing partly about whether your chosen framework can handle this case at
all.** That is not a flaw in the assignment. Noticing where a framework runs out is a substantial part
of learning to use one.

A strong paper will not pretend the difficulty away. The weakest papers on this prompt assert an
obligation and then describe pleasant behaviours; the strongest confront the fact that "you should be
decent" is easy to say and hard to make binding.

## A Note on Writing About This Topic

You may write about a structural problem you have personally experienced. If you do, the paper is
still assessed as an argument — personal testimony can motivate a claim but cannot substitute for
defending it.

You may equally write about a problem you have not experienced. If you do, resist the temptation to
speak for people who have. **Argue about obligations, which is what the prompt asks**, rather than
characterising experiences you are reconstructing.

Neither choice is scored higher than the other.
