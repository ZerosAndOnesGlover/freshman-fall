# Discussion Questions — Week 9 Seminar
### Tech Industry Culture

**Come with written notes.** Participation is assessed on the quality of reasoning.

**A ground rule for this week**, stated in the lecture and repeated here: arguments must be about
evidence and reasoning, and must be made in a way that does not require anyone present to defend
their own presence as a debating position. Everything may be argued; the seminar has to remain a place
where everyone can think.

---

## Part 1: The Historical Puzzle (~15 min)

**1.** Women earned about 37% of US CS degrees in 1984, and the share then fell for twenty years while
rising in medicine, law and biology. **Any adequate explanation has to account for a reversal specific
to computing.** Which of the proposed explanations do you find most plausible, and what evidence would
distinguish between them?

**2.** Abbate argues the field's composition is a historical contingency rather than a natural sorting.
If that is right, what follows practically? If it is wrong, what would we expect to observe that we do
not?

**3.** The lecture separates the **justice** argument for diversity from the **epistemic** one, and
insists they be kept apart. Is that separation useful or evasive? What is lost by keeping them
separate — and what is lost by merging them?

**4.** Suppose a well-designed study found no product-quality benefit from team diversity. **What
exactly would that change?** Answer for each of the two arguments separately.

---

## Part 2: Work Culture (~15 min)

**5.** Sustained crunch reliably fails to produce proportionate output, and this has been known for
decades. **Why does it persist?** Give the most charitable explanation you can, then the least.

**6.** "We're looking for people who are passionate about this." Identify the legitimate thing this
sentence can mean and the illegitimate thing it can do. **Can an employer say it honestly?** If so,
what else would have to be true of the workplace?

**7.** Someone must answer the pager at 3am — that is a genuine operational requirement. So what
distinguishes a well-run on-call rotation from an exploitative one? Try to produce criteria that
would let an outsider tell them apart.

**8.** The lecture proposes a test: *if a workplace's culture would exclude an excellent engineer who
leaves at 5pm, it is selecting for something other than excellence.* Is the test sound? Construct the
strongest objection to it.

---

## Part 3: Individual and Structure (~15 min)

**9.** The lecture claims imposter syndrome can **misdiagnose a structural problem as an individual
one**. Where is the line? Describe a case that is genuinely the individual's cognition and one that is
genuinely the environment, and say how you would tell them apart from inside.

**10.** Pure structuralism is demobilising; pure individualism blames people for conditions they did
not create. **In practice, where do you locate your own obligation** as a junior engineer with no
authority?

**11.** Dobbin and Kalev find that mandatory bias training performs poorly while accountability and
sponsorship perform better. Suppose your future employer runs the training and nothing else.
**What, if anything, do you do about it?**

**12.** The lecture suggests a specific small action: "I want to go back to what she said." It costs
little and is rare. **Why is it rare?** — and is the explanation you give also an excuse?

---

## Closing

**One question to leave with:** think about the workplaces you have been in — jobs, teams, classrooms,
clubs. Identify one person who made it materially better for other people, and one specific thing they
did.

**Almost certainly they had no authority to change anything.** Consider what that tells you about
where the leverage actually is, and whether you intend to use yours.
