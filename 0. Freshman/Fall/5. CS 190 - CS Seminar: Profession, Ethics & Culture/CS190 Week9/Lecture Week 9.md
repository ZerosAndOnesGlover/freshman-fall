## 1. Why a Seminar on Ethics Covers Workplace Culture

The previous five weeks examined harms that software does to people outside the company. This week
examines harms the industry does to the people inside it — and, more importantly, the connection
between the two.

That connection is the reason this is an ethics topic rather than an HR one. **A homogeneous team
building a product for a heterogeneous population makes predictable errors**, and Week 4's
algorithmic bias cases are full of them. **An exhausted engineer ships worse code.** **A workplace
where nobody dares raise a concern has no functioning disclosure process**, whatever Week 8's policy
document says.

Culture is not a soft topic adjacent to the technical work. It is one of the inputs to it.

## 2. The Demographic Picture

### 2.1 The statistic that should surprise you

Women earned roughly **37% of US computer science bachelor's degrees in 1984**. The share then
**declined for two decades**, reaching the high teens by around 2010, and has recovered only partially
since.

**Stop and consider what that rules out.** Every other quantitative field — medicine, law, biology,
mathematics — moved in the opposite direction over the same period. Whatever explains computing's
trajectory has to explain a *reversal* that was specific to this discipline, at a time when women's
participation in comparable fields was rising.

Explanations offered include the arrival of the home computer marketed overwhelmingly to boys, the
consequent shift in what incoming students were assumed to already know, and the hardening of a
particular cultural image of the programmer. None is fully established. What is established is that
**"it was always like this" is false**, which matters, because a decline that happened can in
principle be reversed.

### 2.2 The pipeline metaphor and its limits

The standard framing is a **pipeline**: too few people from underrepresented groups enter the field,
so the fix is recruitment.

The framing is not wrong, but it is badly incomplete, because **attrition is at least as large a
problem as recruitment**. People from underrepresented groups leave technical roles at substantially
higher rates than their peers, and exit surveys point repeatedly at workplace culture rather than
interest or ability.

> **The practical implication:** an organisation that pours resources into hiring while ignoring the
> conditions that cause people to leave is filling a bucket with a hole in it. It will also, quite
> reasonably, be accused of treating diversity as a public-relations metric rather than a commitment.

### 2.3 Two arguments, kept separate

There are two distinct cases for diversity, and conflating them weakens both.

**The justice argument:** people should not face worse prospects in a well-paid, influential
profession because of characteristics irrelevant to the work. This argument stands on its own and
requires no further justification.

**The epistemic argument:** homogeneous teams have correlated blind spots, and correlated blind spots
produce products that fail for populations the team does not resemble. Week 4's cases — facial
analysis systems that failed on darker-skinned faces, hiring tools that learned historical exclusion —
are the evidence.

**Keep them separate.** The epistemic argument is instrumentally useful and empirically contestable.
The justice argument is neither, and it does not become void if a study finds a null result.

## 3. Work Culture

### 3.1 Crunch

**Crunch** is a sustained period of mandatory overtime before a deadline. It is most documented in
games, where it has been the subject of repeated public controversy since the mid-2000s, but it is
not confined there.

The persistent finding — from software engineering research and from the manufacturing literature
before it — is that **sustained overtime does not produce proportionate output.** Productivity per
hour falls, defect rates rise, and the additional hours are substantially spent creating and then
repairing errors. Beyond a few weeks, the total output can fall in absolute terms.

**Which raises the real question:** if crunch does not work, why does it persist?

Plausible answers include that its costs are deferred and diffuse while its appearance of urgency is
immediate and visible; that it is easier to demand hours than to fix estimation; and that
**it is visible effort**, which is rewarded in organisations that cannot measure output directly.

### 3.2 The rhetoric of passion

A distinctive feature of technology work is the expectation that you **love it**.

This has real benefits — the field genuinely does attract people who would program anyway — but it
carries a specific hazard. **"We're looking for people who are passionate"** can function as a
mechanism for extracting unpaid hours, because a passionate person would presumably not object.

It also narrows who fits. Someone with caring responsibilities, or with a life the job does not
occupy, becomes legible as insufficiently committed rather than as a competent professional with a
proportionate relationship to their employer.

> **The test to apply:** if a workplace's stated culture would exclude a demonstrably excellent
> engineer who leaves at 5pm, the culture is selecting for something other than excellence.

### 3.3 On-call and the always-available norm

Operating a service means someone must respond when it breaks at 3am. That is a real requirement, not
a cultural failure.

**What varies enormously is how it is handled.** A well-run rotation has adequate staffing, defined
compensation, protected recovery time, and a serious commitment to reducing alert volume. A badly-run
one has a single overloaded engineer, no compensation, no follow-up on the causes, and normalises
sleep disruption as dedication.

The difference is not technical. It is a decision about who bears the cost of the organisation's
reliability commitments.

## 4. Mental Health

### 4.1 What the conditions produce

The occupational features described above — sustained pressure, blurred boundaries, sleep disruption,
constant visible measurement — are recognisable risk factors for burnout, which the World Health
Organization classified in ICD-11 as an **occupational phenomenon** characterised by exhaustion,
cynical detachment from the job, and reduced efficacy.

**The ICD-11 framing is worth noticing.** Burnout is described as arising from *chronic workplace
stress that has not been successfully managed* — locating the cause in the workplace rather than in
the individual's resilience.

### 4.2 Imposter syndrome, and a caution about the term

The experience of believing oneself a fraud despite evidence of competence was first described by
Clance and Imes in 1978. It is extremely common in this field, and naming it helps people recognise
they are not uniquely afflicted.

**But the term can also do harm**, by relocating a structural problem inside an individual's head. If
you are the only person like you in the room, are spoken over, and have your contributions
misattributed, the belief that you do not belong is not a cognitive distortion — **it is an accurate
reading of your environment.** Calling that imposter syndrome and recommending confidence-building
misdiagnoses it.

The distinction matters because the remedies differ entirely: one calls for individual support, the
other for changing the room.

### 4.3 Help-seeking

Barriers in this industry are specific and identifiable: a strong professional identity built around
intellectual capability, security clearances and immigration statuses that make people wary of any
record, and the reasonable fear that disclosure will affect how one's technical judgement is regarded.

**None of these are irrational.** Advice to "just ask for help" that does not acknowledge them will be
correctly ignored.

## 5. Structural and Individual: Holding Both

The consistent trap in this material is choosing one framing and dismissing the other.

**Pure structuralism** — "the system is the problem, individual choices are irrelevant" — is
demobilising, and it is convenient for anyone who would rather not change their own behaviour.

**Pure individualism** — "just set boundaries, just be resilient" — asks people to solve alone what
the organisation created, and blames them when they cannot.

**Both are needed, and they operate at different scales.** Structures determine the distribution of
outcomes; individual conduct determines what happens in the room you are standing in. A junior
engineer cannot fix an industry, but they can decline to laugh, can attribute an idea to the person
who had it, and can say the quiet thing out loud in a meeting where it costs them something.

**What the evidence supports**, tentatively, is that the interventions that survive scrutiny are
**structural and specific**: structured interviews rather than unstructured ones, transparent
promotion criteria, sponsorship rather than mentoring alone, and measuring **retention** rather than
only hiring. Generic unconscious-bias training, by contrast, has a weak evidence base for changing
behaviour, whatever it does for awareness.

## 6. What This Means for You Specifically

You will spend the next several years as the most junior person in the room, which is the position
with the least power and — for exactly that reason — the clearest view.

Three things worth deciding in advance:

**1. What you will do in the first meeting where someone's idea is misattributed.** The move is
small: "I want to go back to what she said." It costs almost nothing and it is startlingly rare.

**2. What your actual limits are, before you are asked to exceed them.** People who have not decided
in advance discover their limit only by passing it.

**3. Whether you would rather be the person who was there.** Most of what makes a workplace habitable
is done by people with no authority to change policy, who simply behave decently and consistently in
front of others.

## 7. A Note on Disagreement

This week's material is more contested than any other in the course. Some of you will think the
industry's diversity problems are overstated; some will think this lecture badly understates them.

**The seminar can accommodate both, on one condition:** arguments must be about evidence and
reasoning, and must be made in a way that does not require anyone present to defend their own
existence in the room as a debating position. That is not a restriction on what may be argued. It is
the ordinary requirement that a seminar be a place where everyone can actually think.
