# Reading Guide — Week 9
### Tech Industry Culture: Diversity, Work Culture, Mental Health

---

## Required Reading

### 1. Abbate, J. — *Recoding Gender: Women's Changing Participation in Computing* (MIT Press, 2012) — Introduction and Chapter 1

A historian's account of how programming went from being work that women did in substantial numbers
to a field culturally coded as male. Abbate's central move is to treat the present composition of the
field as a **historical outcome** requiring explanation, rather than as a baseline.

**Read for:** the evidence that early programming was gendered *female* precisely because it was
regarded as clerical, and what changed when the work acquired status.

**Guiding questions:**
- What does Abbate identify as the mechanisms by which the field's gender composition shifted?
- If the composition of computing is a historical contingency rather than a natural sorting, what
  follows for how we should interpret the 1984 peak and subsequent decline?
- Whose accounts does Abbate rely on, and what are the limits of that evidence base?

---

### 2. World Health Organization — ICD-11 entry on **burn-out** (QD85), full text of the classification note

Very short — a few paragraphs. Read the actual classification language rather than secondary coverage
of it.

**Read for:** the precise wording. Burnout is classified as an **occupational phenomenon** resulting
from *chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed*, and is explicitly **not**
classified as a medical condition.

**Guiding questions:**
- The definition locates the cause in the workplace. What follows for where responsibility for
  remedy sits?
- Why might the WHO have declined to classify it as a medical condition? What would change if it had?
- The three dimensions are exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced efficacy. Which of these would an
  employer notice first, and which matters most to the person experiencing it?

---

### 3. Clance, P. R. & Imes, S. A. — "The Imposter Phenomenon in High Achieving Women" (*Psychotherapy: Theory, Research & Practice*, 1978)

The original paper. Short, readable, and worth reading in the original because the popular version has
drifted substantially from it.

**Read for:** what the authors actually described, the population they studied, and what they proposed
as remedy.

**Guiding questions:**
- The original study concerned high-achieving women specifically. What is gained and what is lost when
  the concept is generalised to everyone?
- The lecture argues that the term can misdiagnose a structural problem as an individual one. Does the
  original paper support or resist that reading?

## Optional / Enrichment Reading

### 4. Kapor Center — *Tech Leavers Study* (2017)

A survey-based study of why people leave technology roles, notable for asking about **exits** rather
than entries. Read the executive summary and the section on unfair treatment as a driver of attrition.

The methodological caveats are worth attending to — it is self-reported, retrospective, and drawn from
a US sample — but it remains one of the few systematic attempts to measure the leaky end of the
pipeline rather than the intake.

### 5. Bosch, T. — "EA Spouse" and its aftermath — any retrospective account of the 2004 open letter

A single anonymous blog post about working conditions at a games studio that became a landmark in the
industry's discussion of crunch. Useful as a case study in how workplace norms become publicly
contestable.

### 6. Dobbin, F. & Kalev, A. — "Why Diversity Programs Fail" (*Harvard Business Review*, 2016)

An analysis of which corporate diversity interventions show measurable effects and which do not. The
finding that mandatory bias training performs poorly, while structural changes such as accountability
and sponsorship perform better, is the empirical basis for the lecture's §5 claim.

**Read critically.** The evidence base is observational and the authors' framing is contested. It is
included because the seminar should engage with the strongest available evidence, not because it
settles the question.

## A Note on Reading This Week's Material

Some of this reading concerns experiences that people in the seminar have had and others have not.
That asymmetry is real and worth naming.

Read the empirical material as empirical material — asking what was measured, on whom, and how well.
And read it knowing that for some people in the room it is not abstract. **Both of those are ordinary
scholarly obligations**, and they are compatible.
