### Why This Week Exists

Every previous week examined harm that software does to people outside the company. This week
examines the conditions inside it — and the connection between the two, which is why this belongs in
an ethics seminar rather than an induction pack.

The connection is direct. A homogeneous team building for a heterogeneous population makes the
predictable errors of Week 4. An exhausted engineer ships worse code. A workplace where nobody dares
raise a concern has no functioning disclosure process, whatever Week 8's policy says. **Culture is an
input to the technical work, not an adjacent topic.**

This is also the most contested material in the course. Some of you will think the industry's
problems are overstated and some will think this week understates them. The seminar accommodates both,
on the condition stated in the lecture: arguments are about evidence and reasoning, and are made in a
way that does not require anyone present to defend their own presence as a debating position.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of Week 9, you should be able to:

1. State what happened to women's participation in US computer science after 1984, and explain why any adequate account must explain a reversal specific to computing.
2. Explain the limits of the pipeline metaphor and why attrition and recruitment require different remedies.
3. Distinguish the **justice** and **epistemic** arguments for diversity, and say what would follow if the epistemic one failed empirically.
4. Explain why crunch fails to produce proportionate output, and give plausible accounts of why it persists.
5. Identify what "passion" rhetoric can legitimately mean and what it can be used to do.
6. State the ICD-11 characterisation of burnout and explain the significance of locating its cause in the workplace.
7. Explain how the language of imposter syndrome can misdiagnose a structural problem, and distinguish the cases where it does not.
8. Hold structural and individual framings together, and identify which interventions have the better evidence base.

### About Position Paper #3

This is the last and hardest of the three papers, and the sequence is deliberate.

- **Paper #1** gave you an authoritative code (the ACM Code) and asked you to apply it.
- **Paper #2** removed the code and asked you to build a framework for assigning responsibility.
- **Paper #3 removes the identifiable agent**, and asks what an individual owes regarding a harm that
  nobody decided.

Most ethical frameworks were built to evaluate actions by agents, and handle structural harm awkwardly.
You will therefore be arguing partly about **whether your chosen framework can handle the case at
all** — which is not a flaw in the assignment but a large part of learning to use one.

The due date is extended to before **Week 11**, both because the paper is harder and because Week 10's
material on automation and the future of work bears directly on it.

### Connections

**Back:** Week 4's algorithmic bias cases are the evidence for the epistemic argument. Week 8's
disclosure policies depend on a culture in which people will actually invoke them. Week 3's ethical
frameworks are the tools Paper #3 will test to destruction.

**Forward:** Week 10 examines automation and the future of work — structural change on a larger scale,
and a direct extension of this week's question about obligation without leverage. Week 11 is the
student presentations, for which Paper #3 is the natural source material.
