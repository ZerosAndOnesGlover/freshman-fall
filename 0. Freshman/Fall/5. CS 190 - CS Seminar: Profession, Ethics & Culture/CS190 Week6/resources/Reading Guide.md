# Reading Guide — Week 6

## Required Reading

### 1. Asaro, P. — "On Banning Autonomous Weapon Systems: Human Rights, Automation, and the Dehumanization of Lethal Decision-making" (*International Review of the Red Cross*, 2012)
**~20 pages. Free PDF via ICRC or the author's academic page.**

The clearest articulation of the human-dignity-based argument against autonomous weapons — that delegating lethal decisions to machines is wrong independent of accuracy. Read alongside a mental note of the accuracy-based counterargument from Section 2.2 of the lecture, which this paper is directly responding to.

**Guiding questions:**
- Asaro's argument does not depend on autonomous weapons being less accurate than human soldiers. Construct the strongest response a proponent of autonomous weapons could offer to an argument that does not turn on accuracy at all.
- Is there a meaningful difference, in Asaro's framework, between "human-on-the-loop" systems with a nominal human supervisor and fully autonomous ones? Where would he likely draw the line, and do you agree with where he draws it?

### 2. Chesney, B. & Citron, D. — "Deep Fakes: A Looming Challenge for Privacy, Democracy, and National Security" (*California Law Review*, 2019)
**~25 pages (the full paper is longer; focus on the introduction, the "liar's dividend" section, and the policy responses section). Free PDF via SSRN.**

The originating source for the "liar's dividend" concept discussed in the lecture, and a comprehensive legal-scholarship treatment of deepfake harms across categories (this paper predates the more recent NCII volume data, but the analytical framework holds up well).

**Guiding question:**
- Chesney and Citron were writing in 2019, before the more recent explosion of consumer-accessible generative AI tools. Which of their predictions have held up, and which have been overtaken by developments they could not have anticipated (e.g., real-time deepfake video calls)?

### 3. Bender, E., Gebru, T., McMillan-Major, A., Shmitchell, S. — "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big?" (2021)
**~15 pages (read Sections 1–4; the later technical sections are optional). Free PDF widely available (search title; originally published at FAccT 2021).**

A highly influential and also highly contested paper (its publication controversy at Google is itself a case study worth knowing about — one of the paper's authors, Timnit Gebru, was terminated from Google shortly after internal conflict over this paper, an episode you may want to research independently). The paper argues large language models pose risks including environmental cost, encoded bias at scale, and the illusion of understanding where none exists — the "stochastic parrot" framing.

**Guiding questions:**
- The "stochastic parrot" metaphor argues LLMs manipulate linguistic form without access to meaning. Given developments since 2021, do you think this framing still holds, or has model behavior evolved in ways that complicate it? Argue your view directly — this is a genuinely contested question among serious researchers, not one with an agreed answer.
- The paper's publication controversy (an internal review dispute that led to a co-author's termination) is itself relevant to Week 2's discussion of industrial research labs' incentive structures. What does this episode suggest about the tension between corporate-affiliated researchers and publication of findings unflattering to their employer's core product?

## Optional / Enrichment Reading

### 4. UN Office for Disarmament Affairs — "Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons: Group of Governmental Experts on LAWS" (background documents, ongoing)
**Free at un.org.** For students interested in the actual state of international policy negotiation on autonomous weapons — a slower-moving and more consensus-constrained process than the academic literature above, useful for grounding the theoretical debate in the mechanics of how (or whether) international law actually changes.

### 5. Kapoor, S. & Narayanan, A. — "AI Snake Oil" (Substack/book, ongoing)
**Free online.** A skeptical, empirically grounded running critique of overclaimed AI capabilities and risks from both "hype" and "doom" directions — useful as a corrective lens for evaluating claims (including some in this week's lecture) about what current AI systems can and cannot reliably do.
