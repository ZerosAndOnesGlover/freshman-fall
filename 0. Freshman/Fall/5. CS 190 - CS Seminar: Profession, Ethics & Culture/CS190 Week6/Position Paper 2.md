## The Prompt

Week 4's algorithmic bias content examined cases where a system's *malfunction* — bias in training data, poorly chosen proxies, unrepresentative sampling — produced unintended harm. The remedy in those cases was, at least in principle, better engineering: fix the data, fix the objective, disaggregate the metrics.

This week's content examined a different structure: cases where a system operating *exactly as designed* — a perfectly accurate autonomous weapon, a perfectly convincing deepfake, a perfectly fluent (if occasionally hallucinating) LLM — remains the subject of live ethical dispute. Better engineering does not resolve these disputes, because the disagreement is not about whether the system works, but about whether its correct function is itself legitimate, and who bears responsibility for its use.

**Your task:** Choose *one* of the following three technologies — autonomous weapons systems, deepfake generation tools, or large language models — and take and defend a specific position on the following question, applied to your chosen technology:

*When a technology's core, correctly-functioning capability is itself ethically contested, what obligation (if any) do the engineers who build it bear for how it is subsequently used — and does that obligation differ from the obligation an engineer bears when a system malfunctions?*

Your paper must do all of the following:

1. **State a clear, specific position** in your opening paragraph regarding your chosen technology. Do not attempt to cover all three technologies — depth on one is required, not breadth across all.
2. **Engage explicitly with the accountability/responsibility question**, not just the harm itself. It is not enough to argue that a given technology is harmful or beneficial — you must take a position on *who is responsible* when it causes harm, and why.
3. **Address the strongest counterargument to your position.** If you argue engineers bear significant responsibility for downstream misuse of a general-purpose tool, engage with the strongest version of the "guns don't kill people" style objection (tools are morally neutral; responsibility lies entirely with the user). If you argue engineers bear little responsibility, engage with the strongest version of the argument that foreseeable, serious, common misuse patterns create a design obligation regardless of user intent.
4. **Reference at least one specific case or framework from this week's lecture or readings** — the accountability gap in LAWS, the liar's dividend, the stochastic parrots critique, or a specific documented case (the UK voice-cloning fraud, a sanctioned LLM court filing, a specific autonomous weapons deployment).
5. **Reach a conclusion with practical implications** for how an engineer in your chosen field should actually behave — what they should build, refuse to build, or build differently.

## What Distinguishes a Strong Paper Here From Position Paper #1

Position Paper #1 asked you to reason about an individual engineer's obligations under a specific, textually explicit code (the ACM Code of Ethics). This paper asks you to reason without that scaffold — there is no single authoritative code that resolves the autonomous weapons, deepfake, or LLM responsibility questions the way the ACM Code at least partially addressed Paper #1's scenarios. You will need to construct your own framework for assigning responsibility, drawing on the ethical reasoning tools introduced across the semester (consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics from Week 3's glossary; the ACM Code's principles where they do apply) rather than relying on a single authoritative source to do the work for you.

A strong paper will therefore likely need to *build* an argument about responsibility from more basic principles, rather than citing a rule that settles the matter. This is harder than Paper #1, and is meant to be — the position papers are designed to increase in difficulty across the semester.

## A Note on Scope Discipline

Papers that attempt to address all three technologies (LAWS, deepfakes, LLMs) in 1,000 words will almost always be superficial on all three rather than rigorous on one. Choose one. If you find yourself wanting to discuss all three, that impulse is a signal to go deeper on your chosen case, not to add breadth — use the other two only as brief comparative touchpoints if genuinely useful to sharpen your argument, not as separate sections of equal weight.
