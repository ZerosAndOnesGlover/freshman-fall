# Discussion Questions — Week 6 Seminar

---

## Part 1: Autonomous Weapons (~15 min)

1. The lecture presents three arguments about autonomous weapons: the harm-reduction case (machines could be more law-compliant than frightened, angry humans), the accountability-gap case, and the human-dignity case. Which argument do you find strongest, and can you construct a response to it from a proponent of autonomous weapons that you find genuinely difficult to answer?

2. The accountability gap argument holds that existing frameworks (command responsibility, courts-martial) do not map cleanly onto autonomous systems. Propose a specific accountability mechanism that could work for a human-out-of-the-loop system. Does your proposal actually solve the problem, or does it just relocate it?

3. Is there a principled distinction between a landmine (a "dumb" autonomous weapon that has existed for over a century, indiscriminately triggered by anyone including civilians, and is banned by international treaty for exactly that reason) and a modern AI-driven LAWS that can, in principle, distinguish combatants from civilians? Does the existence of the landmine ban support or undermine the case for a LAWS ban?

## Part 2: Deepfakes (~15 min)

4. The lecture states that non-consensual intimate imagery constitutes the majority of deepfake content by volume, not political disinformation. Why do you think public and policy discourse about deepfakes disproportionately focuses on political/electoral harms rather than NCII? What does this discrepancy reveal about whose harms get prioritized in technology policy discussions?

5. The "liar's dividend" argument holds that deepfakes harm society even when no one is actually deceived by a specific fake — because the mere existence of convincing fakes lets guilty parties dismiss real evidence as fabricated. Is this a compelling independent harm, or is it a second-order effect too speculative to weigh heavily in policy decisions?

6. Content provenance approaches (C2PA) shift the burden from "prove this is fake" to "prove this is authentic." What are the practical limits of this approach? Who is disadvantaged by a system that treats unsigned/unverified media as presumptively suspect?

## Part 3: LLMs (~15 min)

7. Several documented court cases involve lawyers submitting filings with fabricated LLM-generated citations. Whose failure is this — the lawyer's, for not verifying output, or a foreseeable consequence of a tool that is, per the "stochastic parrot" argument, incapable of guaranteeing factual grounding? Does assigning responsibility to the human user let the technology's design off the hook, or is that the correct allocation of responsibility?

8. The lecture distinguishes "task automation" from "job elimination" and notes this distinction is often blurred in public discussion. Pick a specific job or task you're familiar with and analyze it through this lens: what parts of it are being automated as discrete tasks, and does that add up to elimination of the job as a whole, a transformation of it, or something else?

9. The lecture takes an explicitly neutral position on AI existential risk debates, noting serious researchers disagree sharply. Is neutrality the right stance for this seminar to take, or does declining to take a position itself constitute an implicit position (e.g., "the risk is not serious enough to warrant urgency")? Argue either that neutrality is appropriate here or that it isn't.

## Closing

10. Across all three topics this week, identify one place where you think "capability outpacing governance" (the lecture's unifying frame) is likely to be resolved by law and policy catching up within the next five years, and one place where you think the gap will persist much longer. Justify the difference.
