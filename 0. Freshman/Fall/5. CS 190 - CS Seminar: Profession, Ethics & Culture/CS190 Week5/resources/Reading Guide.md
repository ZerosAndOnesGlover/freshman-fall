# Reading Guide — Week 5

---

## Required Reading

### 1. Solove, D. — "A Taxonomy of Privacy" (*University of Pennsylvania Law Review*, 2006)
**~15 pages (read the introduction and the taxonomy overview; the detailed sub-sections on each harm category are optional deep-dives). Free PDF widely available via law review archives or SSRN.**

The foundational text for this week's privacy vocabulary. Solove's central move is rejecting a single unified definition of privacy in favor of a taxonomy of distinct harmful activities. Read for the structure of the argument as much as the content.

**Guiding questions:**
- Solove argues that trying to define privacy as one unified concept has failed repeatedly in legal scholarship. Why might a taxonomy of harms be more useful for engineers specifically, compared to lawyers or philosophers?
- Pick two categories from the taxonomy (e.g., aggregation and secondary use) and identify a system you're familiar with that could produce each harm, even if it currently doesn't.

### 2. Zuboff, S. — *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*, Introduction (excerpt)
**~25 pages. University library, or widely excerpted online ("Zuboff surveillance capitalism introduction").**

Read for the core argument: that human experience is treated as raw material, extracted as "behavioral surplus," and sold as predictions. You do not need to read the full 700-page book — the introduction lays out the thesis clearly.

**Guiding question:**
- Zuboff's critics argue her framework treats all data collection as inherently sinister and cannot distinguish between genuinely harmful practices and mutually beneficial value exchanges. After reading, do you think this criticism lands? Construct the strongest version of both Zuboff's argument and this counterargument.

### 3. *Carpenter v. United States*, 585 U.S. \_\_\_ (2018) — Majority Opinion (Roberts, C.J.)
**~15 pages of the opinion (the full case including dissents is longer; focus on the majority). Free at supremecourt.gov or oyez.org (which also has a very good case summary and oral argument audio).**

Read the majority opinion directly rather than only a summary — the reasoning about *why* cell-site location data is different from the bank records and phone records in prior third-party doctrine cases is the important part, not just the outcome.

**Guiding questions:**
- The majority opinion goes out of its way to describe the decision as "narrow." What does the Court explicitly decline to decide? Why might a court want to rule narrowly on a question with such broad technological implications?
- Justice Gorsuch's dissent (optional but recommended if time allows) argues for a completely different Fourth Amendment framework based on property law rather than "reasonable expectations." What would change about privacy law if his approach were adopted instead?

## Optional / Enrichment Reading

### 4. GDPR — Articles 5, 6, 17, and 25 (official text)
**Free at gdpr-info.eu.** Reading the actual regulatory text (not a summary) for the core principles, lawful basis for processing, right to erasure, and privacy by design/default. Shorter and more directly useful for engineering purposes than it might sound.

### 5. Kerr, O. — "The Case for the Third-Party Doctrine" (*Michigan Law Review*, 2009)
**Free PDF via SSRN.** A rigorous defense of the third-party doctrine that predates *Carpenter* — useful if you want to understand the strongest argument *for* the doctrine this week's lecture is otherwise fairly critical of.
