# Reading Guide — Week 2

## Required Reading

### 1. Beck, K. et al. — "Manifesto for Agile Software Development" (agilemanifesto.org) + "Twelve Principles Behind the Agile Manifesto"
**~2 pages total. Free at agilemanifesto.org.**

Yes, it's short. Read it twice. The first time for content; the second time to notice what it *doesn't* say, and what assumptions it bakes in without stating them.

**Guiding questions:**
- The manifesto values "individuals and interactions over processes and tools." Is this actually what happens in Agile as practiced in most companies? What's the gap between the document and the practice?
- Principle 5: "Build projects around motivated individuals. Give them the environment and support they need, and trust them to get the job done." What organizational structures make this genuinely difficult to implement, regardless of intent?

### 2. Raymond, E. — "The Cathedral and the Bazaar" (1997/1999)
**~30 pages. Free at catb.org/esr/writings/cathedral-bazaar/.**

Eric Raymond's influential essay contrasting the closed, hierarchical development model ("the cathedral") with the open, distributed open-source model ("the bazaar"). Introduced the aphorism "given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow" (Linus's Law). Hugely influential in the late 1990s in legitimizing open source to corporate decision-makers.

**Guiding questions:**
- Raymond argues that open source produces more reliable software because more people review the code. The xz Utils backdoor (described in this week's lecture) was a long-term, sophisticated social engineering attack by a malicious contributor. Does this case falsify Raymond's argument, complicate it, or neither?
- Raymond's essay is explicitly a piece of advocacy. What rhetorical moves does he make that a more neutral analysis might not? Where does he acknowledge counterevidence, and where does he not?

### 3. Eghbal, N. — *Working in Public: The Making and Maintenance of Open Source Software*, Chapter 1
**~25 pages. University library.**

Nadia Eghbal's 2020 analysis of the sustainability crisis in open source — who actually maintains open source projects, under what conditions, and why the current model is structurally strained. More empirically grounded and more recent than Raymond.

**Guiding questions:**
- Eghbal distinguishes between different types of open source projects by their contributor/user ratio. What does this taxonomy reveal that Raymond's cathedral/bazaar dichotomy misses?
- If you built a widely-used open source library and it became critical infrastructure, what obligations (if any) do you think you would have to maintain it? What obligations do its users have to support you?

## Optional / Enrichment Reading

### 4. Royce, W. — "Managing the Development of Large Software Systems" (1970)
**Free PDF, widely available. ~10 pages.**
The original waterfall paper — read it to see how badly it has been misread by everyone who cites it as the "waterfall" model.

### 5. Forsgren, N., Humble, J., Kim, G. — *Accelerate*, Chapter 2 ("Measuring Software Delivery Performance")
**University library.**
The research foundation behind DORA metrics — the most rigorous empirical study of what software development practices actually predict engineering performance. If you want to ground Agile claims in data rather than ideology, start here.
