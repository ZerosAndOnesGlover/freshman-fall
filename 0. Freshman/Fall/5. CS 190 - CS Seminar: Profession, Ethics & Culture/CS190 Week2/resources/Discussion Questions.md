# Discussion Questions — Week 2 Seminar

---

## Part 1: Development Process (~15 min)

1. The lecture argues that Agile's core insight is that requirements change and cannot be fully specified upfront, and that this is an *empirical fact* about software, not a failure of discipline. Do you buy this? Can you construct a domain where upfront specification is actually feasible — where waterfall would be the better model? What makes that domain different?

2. Velocity, story points, and sprint metrics — all originated as internal team tools — are routinely used by management to compare and evaluate engineers. The Agile Manifesto explicitly opposed management using such metrics for surveillance. Is this an Agile implementation failure, or was it a predictable outcome of introducing quantitative metrics into a management context? What does Goodhart's Law predict here?

3. The lecture describes "cargo-cult Agile" — adopting the rituals (standups, sprints, retrospectives) without the underlying philosophy (trust, autonomy, responding to change). How would you distinguish, from the outside, between a team genuinely practicing Agile and one performing it? What would the observable difference be?

## Part 2: Open Source (~15 min)

4. Richard Stallman's GPL was designed to prevent a commons from being enclosed — you can use GPL code, but you must give back. Eric Raymond's "bazaar" model emphasizes practical benefits (better software). Nadia Eghbal's analysis focuses on sustainability (the commons is being consumed without maintenance). These are three different *framings* of open source. Which framing do you find most analytically useful, and why? (Note: "most accurate" and "most useful" may not be the same answer.)

5. The xz Utils backdoor involved a malicious actor who spent approximately two years building trust as a legitimate contributor before introducing a backdoor. The detection was accidental. What systemic changes to open source governance would make this kind of attack harder? Be specific — not "better security" but *what specific mechanism* would have caught or prevented this?

6. A company builds a profitable SaaS product on top of open source infrastructure — Linux, PostgreSQL, OpenSSL, dozens of libraries — and contributes nothing back. Is this a moral failure, a rational business decision that the license explicitly permits, or something in between? Does your answer change if the company is a startup vs. a company with a $50B market cap?

## Part 3: Research and Institutional Dynamics (~10 min)

7. The lecture notes that Google published the Transformer architecture (the "Attention Is All You Need" paper, 2017), which competitors — including OpenAI — then used to build products that now compete with Google. OpenAI has moved toward not publishing recent model details. From a purely self-interested perspective, Google's publication looks like a strategic mistake. From a broader societal perspective, it looks like a contribution to the commons. Is there a principled argument for *why* companies with significant research advantages should publish, beyond "it's good for the field"? Or is publication fundamentally altruistic?

8. The "dual-use" problem in research: a paper describing a new technique for finding SQL injection vulnerabilities teaches both defenders and attackers. A paper on AI persuasion capabilities teaches both marketers and propagandists. Should researchers consider likely use distribution when deciding whether to publish? If so, how? Who makes that judgment, and by what standard?

## Closing

9. You will spend most of your career inside one or more of these institutional contexts — startup, large tech company, open source project, research lab, government. Which context do you think would impose the most ethical pressure on you personally, and why?
