# CS 190 — Week 10 Reading Guide
## The Future of Work: Automation, Jobs, Universal Basic Income

**Total required reading: roughly 60 pages.** Two of the three are economics papers written for a
general audience; read them for the *argument structure*, not the regressions.

---

## Required

### 1. David H. Autor, "Why Are There Still So Many Jobs? The History and Future of Workplace Automation"
*Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 29(3), 2015, pp. 3–30.

The single most useful thing you will read this week, and freely available from the JEP archive.
Autor explains why automation both substitutes for and complements labour, introduces Polanyi's
paradox as the constraint on automating tacit skill, and gives the ATM case in its correct form.

> **Guiding question:** Autor's argument rests on the claim that machines complement human labour in
> tasks requiring tacit knowledge. Machine learning does not require a rule to be articulable. **How
> much of Autor's argument survives that development, and which parts specifically?**

---

### 2. Carl Benedikt Frey and Michael A. Osborne, "The Future of Employment: How Susceptible Are Jobs to Computerisation?"
*Technological Forecasting and Social Change*, 114, 2017, pp. 254–280. (Circulated as an Oxford
Martin working paper, 2013.)

**Read the introduction, the methodology section, and the conclusion.** You may skim the appendix of
occupation-by-occupation probabilities, though it is worth finding your own intended career in it.

This is the source of the famous 47% figure. Read it specifically to establish **what the paper
actually claims** — the claim is narrower and more careful than its reception.

> **Guiding question:** Write down, in one sentence, exactly what the 47% refers to. Then write down
> the sentence you have most often heard it reported as. **The gap between those two sentences is
> this week's lesson in reading evidence.**

---

### 3. Kela (Social Insurance Institution of Finland), *Results of Finland's Basic Income Experiment*
Final report summary, 2020. Roughly 10 pages.

The best-documented recent trial. Read the design section as carefully as the results — the design is
what determines what the results can support.

> **Guiding question:** Participants were drawn **only from the unemployed** and the payment ran for
> **two years**. State one conclusion the study supports well, and one conclusion people commonly
> draw from it that the design cannot support.

---

## Optional

### 4. Melanie Arntz, Terry Gregory, Ulrich Zierahn, "The Risk of Automation for Jobs in OECD Countries"
OECD Social, Employment and Migration Working Paper No. 189, 2016.

The task-based reanalysis that yields ~9% instead of ~47%. Read this if you want to see how a
methodological choice about the unit of analysis produces a fivefold difference in a headline number.

### 5. John Maynard Keynes, "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" (1930)
About 12 pages, and a pleasure to read. Written in the Depression, predicting fifteen-hour weeks by
2030. Read it to see a first-rate mind get the productivity right and the sociology wrong.

### 6. Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo, "Robots and Jobs: Evidence from US Labor Markets"
*Journal of Political Economy*, 128(6), 2020.

Technical, but the abstract and introduction are accessible. The rare study that **measures** rather
than forecasts, and finds real but modest, geographically concentrated negative effects.

### 7. Philippe Van Parijs and Yannick Vanderborght, *Basic Income: A Radical Proposal for a Free Society and a Sane Economy*
Harvard University Press, 2017. Chapter 1.

The philosophical case, grounded in "real freedom" rather than in automation. Useful precisely because
it shows the UBI argument does not depend on any forecast about jobs.

---

## How to Read This Week

The temptation is to arrive with a view about UBI and to sort the readings into supporting and
opposing. **Resist it.** The readings are not primarily about UBI; they are about how to hold a
position on a contested empirical question.

Bring to the seminar: **one number you now distrust, and the reason.**
