### Why This Week Exists

Every other topic in this course concerned harm you might do to other people. This one is different
in an uncomfortable way: **you are the ones building the automation, and the people whose work it
displaces are not in the room.**

That asymmetry distorts reasoning in a predictable direction. From inside the profession capturing
the gains, it is cheap to conclude the gains justify the losses. This week's discipline is to reach a
position by consulting the evidence rather than by occupying a vantage point.

The evidence is genuinely contested. Serious economists using different methods produce estimates
differing by a factor of five — not because anyone is dishonest, but because of a methodological
choice about whether the unit of analysis is an occupation or a task. Learning to hold a position
while acknowledging that is most of the week's work.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of Week 10, you should be able to:

1. Describe the Luddite risings accurately, and explain what the **Engels' pause** shows about who bears the cost of a transition and for how long.
2. Explain what Keynes got right and wrong in 1930, and why the failure is more instructive than a success would have been.
3. State precisely what Frey and Osborne's 47% refers to, and explain why a task-based analysis yields roughly 9% from comparable data.
4. Distinguish **substitution** from **complementation**, and give the ATM case in its correct form — including the post-2010 decline.
5. Explain the routine-task hypothesis, why it predicts **polarisation** rather than mass unemployment, and how machine learning weakens the protection Polanyi's paradox afforded.
6. Define universal basic income by its three conditions, and separate the five distinct arguments for it.
7. Say what the Finland, Alaska and GiveDirectly results support, and what they **cannot** support, given their designs.
8. State a position on obligations owed to displaced workers in a form that still holds when the displaced worker is you.

### The Central Skill This Week

Not "what will happen to jobs" — nobody knows. The skill is **reading a contested empirical
literature without collapsing it into a side.**

Concretely: given two credible estimates differing fivefold, you should be able to locate the
disagreement in a specific methodological choice rather than in the honesty or politics of the
authors. That capacity generalises far beyond this topic, and it is what the prep assignment drills.

The closing exercise makes the standard explicit. **A prediction with no falsifier attached is a
mood.** Say what you think will happen, then say what would have to be observed for you to conclude
you were wrong.
