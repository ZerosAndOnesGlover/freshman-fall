## 1. Why This Is the Hardest Week to Think Clearly About

Every previous topic in this course concerned harms you might cause to other people. This one is
different in an uncomfortable way: **you are the ones building the automation**, and the people whose
work it displaces are not in this room.

That asymmetry does something to reasoning. It is easy, from inside the profession that captures the
gains, to arrive at the conclusion that the gains justify the losses. It is equally easy, from
outside, to conclude that the losses are catastrophic and the gains illusory. Both conclusions are
available cheaply, and both are usually reached before the evidence is consulted.

The evidence, when consulted, turns out to be genuinely contested — not "contested" in the sense that
one side is lying, but in the sense that serious economists using different methods get answers that
differ by a factor of five. Part of this week's work is learning to hold a position while
acknowledging that.

There is also a discipline problem specific to this topic: **almost every confident prediction about
technological unemployment made in the last two hundred years has been wrong**, in both directions.
That should not make you a fatalist about prediction. It should make you specific about what your
prediction would look like if it were false.

## 2. The Two-Hundred-Year Argument

### 2.1 The Luddites, correctly described

The Luddites broke machines in the English Midlands between 1811 and 1816. The word now means
someone irrationally hostile to technology, and this is a slander that survived because the winners
wrote it down.

The Luddites were **skilled textile workers** — framework knitters, croppers, weavers — and their
objection was not to machinery as such. It was to machinery deployed to produce inferior goods using
unapprenticed labour at wages that destroyed an existing craft settlement. They had a specific,
articulable grievance about **how** a technology was being introduced, and about a bargain in which
they bore all the cost.

They also lost, comprehensively, and were hanged and transported for it.

Two things are true simultaneously: the mechanisation of textiles enormously increased output and
eventually raised living standards, and the specific people who broke the machines were substantially
correct about what would happen to them. Economic historians call the intervening period the
**Engels' pause** — roughly 1790 to 1840, during which output per worker in Britain rose while real
wages for workers barely moved.

**The gains were real. The delay was around two generations.** Anyone who tells you technological
change is painless is ignoring the pause; anyone who tells you it never pays off is ignoring what
came after it. The interesting question is who bears the cost of the interval, and that question is
political rather than technical.

### 2.2 Keynes, and what he got right

In 1930, in the depths of the Depression, Keynes wrote *Economic Possibilities for our
Grandchildren*, predicting that within a hundred years productivity would rise enough that his
grandchildren would work **fifteen-hour weeks** and the central human problem would be what to do
with leisure.

The productivity forecast was roughly right. The hours forecast was badly wrong.

This failure is more instructive than a success would have been. Keynes assumed that once material
needs were met, people would stop trading time for money. What he did not anticipate was that **wants
expand**, that consumption is partly positional (what counts as enough depends on what others have),
and that the gains would distribute unevenly enough that many people did not experience the
abundance he projected. **The productivity arrived; the leisure did not.**

Hold onto this. The standard optimistic argument about automation is that it makes everyone richer.
Keynes shows that "richer in aggregate" and "materially better off in the way one hoped" are
different claims.

## 3. What the Evidence Actually Says

### 3.1 The 47% number, and why you should stop citing it the way people cite it

In 2013, Carl Benedikt Frey and Michael Osborne circulated an Oxford Martin School working paper,
*The Future of Employment*, later published in *Technological Forecasting and Social Change* (2017).
Its most quoted finding: about **47% of total US employment is in occupations at high risk of
computerisation** over "perhaps a decade or two."

That number went around the world. It is also routinely misreported. Frey and Osborne did **not**
predict that 47% of jobs would disappear. They estimated the share of employment in occupations whose
tasks are, in principle, susceptible to automation given the technology then foreseeable. Whether
automation *happens* depends on cost, regulation, organisational inertia, and whether anyone bothers.

In 2016, Melanie Arntz, Terry Gregory and Ulrich Zierahn published an OECD working paper redoing the
analysis at the level of **tasks rather than occupations** — recognising that jobs are bundles of
tasks, and that automating some tasks in a job usually changes the job rather than deleting it.
Their figure across 21 OECD countries was around **9%**.

**A factor of five, from the same underlying data, because of a methodological choice about the unit
of analysis.** You should find that clarifying rather than discouraging. It tells you exactly where
the disagreement lives: not in whether machines can do things, but in whether jobs decompose the way
the pessimistic model assumes.

### 3.2 Automation as substitution *and* complementation

David Autor's 2015 paper *Why Are There Still So Many Jobs?* makes the point the headline numbers
obscure: automation does two things at once. It **substitutes** for labour in some tasks and
**complements** it in others, raising the value of the tasks that remain.

His standing example is the automated teller machine. ATMs took over cash handling, the single most
routine part of a bank teller's job. Teller employment in the United States did not collapse; it was
roughly flat and then grew modestly for two decades afterwards. The mechanism, as James Bessen
describes it, is that ATMs cut the cost of operating a branch, banks responded by opening more
branches, and the teller's job shifted away from counting cash toward sales and customer
relationships.

**This is not a happy-ending story.** It is a story about *composition*: the number of jobs held up
while the content of the job changed completely, which is excellent news if you can make the
transition and irrelevant if you cannot. Teller employment has in fact declined since around 2010,
as online banking cut branch counts. The ATM story is a story about a delay, not a permanent
exemption.

### 3.3 Routine-task-biased change and the hollowing middle

The framework underneath this is the **routine-task hypothesis** (Autor, Levy and Murnane, 2003).
Automation historically displaced tasks that are *routine* — specifiable in explicit rules — whether
they were manual (assembly) or cognitive (bookkeeping, filing, clerical processing). It did **not**
displace non-routine tasks at either end: high-end analytical and creative work, or low-end manual
work requiring physical dexterity and situational judgement.

Routine jobs cluster in the **middle** of the wage distribution. So the predicted effect is not mass
unemployment but **polarisation** — growth at the top and bottom, hollowing in the middle — and that
is broadly what has been observed in the US and much of Europe since the 1980s.

The constraint on further automation was named by Autor after Michael Polanyi: **"we know more than
we can tell."** Many tasks that look simple resist automation because nobody can articulate the rule
being followed. **This is the part of the picture that machine learning has changed**, because
learned models do not require the rule to be articulable — which is precisely why the current wave
reaches tasks the routine-task hypothesis classified as safe.

### 3.4 The evidence on robots specifically

Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo's *Robots and Jobs: Evidence from US Labor Markets* (2020) is
worth knowing because it is one of the few studies that measures rather than forecasts. Exploiting
variation in industrial robot adoption across US commuting zones, they estimate that each additional
robot per thousand workers reduces the local employment-to-population ratio by roughly **0.2
percentage points** and wages by roughly **0.4%**.

Two features matter. First, the effects are **negative and statistically detectable** — this is not
a study that finds automation harmless. Second, they are **modest in magnitude** compared with the
rhetoric, and **highly concentrated geographically**. Automation's costs are local even when its
benefits are national, which is a large part of why the political response is more intense than the
aggregate numbers seem to warrant.

## 4. Universal Basic Income

### 4.1 What it is, precisely

A **universal basic income** is a regular cash payment to every individual, **unconditional** (no
work or job-search requirement), **universal** (not means-tested), and **individual** (paid to
persons, not households).

Those three properties do most of the argumentative work, and most proposals labelled "UBI" violate
at least one. A means-tested payment is a negative income tax, not a UBI. A payment conditional on
job-seeking is unemployment benefit. **Insisting on the definition is not pedantry** — nearly every
empirical study you will encounter tested something that failed at least one condition, which
directly limits what it can tell you.

### 4.2 The arguments for, which are not all the same argument

- **Automation insurance.** If technological change destroys income-earning opportunity faster than
  it creates it, a floor decoupled from employment maintains demand and dignity. Note that this
  argument is **conditional on an empirical claim that may be false** — if section 3 is right that
  jobs change rather than vanish, this argument weakens considerably.
- **Administrative simplicity.** Means-testing is expensive to run, intrusive, and produces errors in
  both directions. Universality eliminates the apparatus.
- **Bargaining power.** A worker who can refuse a job without destitution can negotiate. This
  argument is independent of automation entirely.
- **Freedom.** Philip Van Parijs argues for "real freedom" — that formal liberty means little without
  the material means to exercise it. Also independent of automation.
- **Recognition of unpaid work.** Care work, largely done by women, is economically essential and
  uncompensated. A universal payment recognises it without administering a test for it.

**These are five different arguments.** The automation argument is the one you will hear from
technologists, and it is the most fragile, because it rests on a forecast. The bargaining-power and
freedom arguments do not depend on automation at all, and would stand even if employment stayed
exactly as it is.

### 4.3 The arguments against, taken seriously

- **Cost.** A meaningful UBI in a rich country runs to a very large fraction of existing government
  spending. Proposals typically fund it by replacing existing transfers — which means some current
  recipients of targeted support **lose**, since a flat payment is less than what the most in need
  currently receive. The distributional question inside a UBI is not automatically progressive.
- **Labour supply.** Standard theory predicts some reduction in hours worked. Whether this is a bug
  or a feature depends on your view of the value of the foregone work.
- **Inflation and incidence.** If a payment is universal and supply of something inelastic — housing
  in a constrained market being the obvious case — some of it is captured by landlords rather than
  recipients.
- **The political economy objection, from both directions.** The left worries UBI is a Trojan horse
  for dismantling public services; the right worries it entrenches permanent dependency. That both
  fear it is not evidence it is correct.

### 4.4 What the experiments show, and what they cannot

**Finland (2017–2018).** 2,000 randomly selected unemployed people received €560/month
unconditionally for two years. Results: employment effects small and modestly positive by the second
year; **substantial and consistent improvements in self-reported wellbeing, mental health, trust,
and financial security.** The most-quoted finding is the wellbeing one, and it is the most robust.

**Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend (since 1982).** Not a UBI in size, but genuinely universal and
unconditional, and running for four decades. Damon Jones and Ioana Marinescu found **no significant
effect on aggregate employment**, with a small increase in part-time work. Its political durability
is itself a finding: universality appears to create a constituency that means-tested programmes lack.

**GiveDirectly, Kenya.** Long-horizon unconditional transfers in low-income villages, with results
generally showing increased assets, earnings and psychological wellbeing.

**The crucial methodological caveat:** none of these is a test of a UBI. Finland's was restricted to
the unemployed and lasted two years. Alaska's is far too small to live on. Kenya's is in an economy
structurally unlike a rich one. **A UBI's most important effects are general-equilibrium ones** —
what happens to wages, prices and social norms when *everyone* has it, permanently — and a
randomised trial on a subset of a population **cannot** measure those by construction. Anyone
claiming an experiment has settled the question is overreading it, in whichever direction.

## 5. What This Has to Do With You

Three connections, and the third is the one that matters this week.

**First: you will be asked to build it.** Not "automation" abstractly — a specific system that does
a specific job someone currently holds. Week 3's frameworks apply to that decision, and the honest
answer is that building it is usually permissible. The question is whether anything else is required
of you alongside it.

**Second: this wave reaches your own field.** Previous waves automated other people's work, and the
profession commenting on them was safely outside. Large language models write code. Whatever you
conclude about obligations owed to displaced workers, you should be able to state it in a form that
still holds when the displaced worker is you. **A principle that changes when you are the subject is
not a principle.**

**Third, and this is the connection to Position Paper #3:** the displacement of work by automation is
the structural harm *par excellence*. No one decides it. It emerges from millions of individually
rational decisions, each defensible, and the aggregate outcome is something no participant chose. The
futility argument is fully available: *I did not cause this, I cannot stop it, my refusal to build it
changes nothing except that someone else builds it.*

That argument is stronger here than anywhere else in this course. If your paper's account of
obligation survives contact with this case, it is a serious account. **If it collapses here, it was
never doing any work** — it was only ever telling you to be nice in situations where being nice was
easy.

## 6. For Next Week

Week 11 is your presentations. Bring the argument from your position paper, not a summary of a topic.

**A closing note on prediction.** Roy Amara's rule of thumb — that we overestimate the effect of a
technology in the short run and underestimate it in the long run — is the most useful thing anyone
has said about this subject, precisely because it forbids both of the cheap positions. The confident
catastrophist and the confident dismisser are making the same error at different time horizons.

Say what you think will happen. Then say what would have to be observed for you to conclude you were
wrong. If nothing would, you have expressed a mood rather than a forecast.
