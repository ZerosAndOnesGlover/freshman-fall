# Glossary — Week 10 Additions

---

**Amara's law** — Roy Amara's observation that we tend to overestimate a technology's effect in the
short run and underestimate it in the long run. Useful because it rules out **both** cheap positions:
the confident catastrophist and the confident dismisser make the same error at different horizons.

**Complementation** — Where automation *raises* the value of human labour in the tasks it does not
take over, by making those tasks more productive or more in demand. The counterpart to
**substitution**; the two occur simultaneously, which is why net employment effects are hard to sign
in advance.

**Engels' pause** — Robert Allen's term for the period of roughly 1790–1840 in Britain during which
output per worker rose substantially while real wages stagnated. Evidence that the gains from
technological change can be real and yet arrive two generations after the costs.

**General-equilibrium effect** — An effect that appears only when a change applies to the whole
system: what happens to wages, prices and social norms when *everyone* receives a basic income,
rather than a randomly selected 2,000 people. **Randomised trials cannot measure these by
construction**, which is the central limitation of every UBI experiment run to date.

**Job polarisation** — Employment growth at the top and bottom of the wage distribution with
hollowing in the middle, predicted by the routine-task hypothesis and broadly observed in the US and
Europe since the 1980s. Note that this is a *compositional* claim, not a claim about total employment.

**Luddite** — Historically, a skilled English textile worker involved in machine-breaking between
1811 and 1816. **The modern pejorative sense is a distortion**: the objection was to a specific
deployment of machinery that undercut craft wages using unapprenticed labour, not to technology as
such.

**Negative income tax** — A means-tested cash transfer that tapers as earned income rises. Frequently
confused with UBI; it fails the **universality** condition, which matters for administrative cost,
take-up, and political durability.

**Polanyi's paradox** — After Michael Polanyi: *"we know more than we can tell."* Many human
competences cannot be articulated as explicit rules, which historically protected them from
automation. **Learned models weaken this protection**, since they do not require the rule to be
stated.

**Routine task** — A task specifiable in explicit rules, whether manual or cognitive. The
**routine-task hypothesis** (Autor, Levy and Murnane, 2003) holds that automation displaces routine
tasks specifically, which explains polarisation better than a simple skill-based account.

**Substitution** — Where a machine performs a task previously performed by a person, reducing demand
for that labour. Only half the story; see **complementation**.

**Task-based analysis** — Estimating automation exposure by decomposing jobs into constituent tasks
rather than treating occupations as atomic. Yields roughly 9% high-risk employment across OECD
countries versus roughly 47% from the occupation-based approach — **the same phenomenon, a different
unit of analysis**.

**Technological unemployment** — Keynes's term (1930) for unemployment caused by the discovery of
labour-saving methods outrunning the discovery of new uses for labour. He regarded it as a temporary
phase of maladjustment; whether it is temporary is the whole argument.

**Universal basic income (UBI)** — A regular cash payment that is **unconditional** (no work or
job-search requirement), **universal** (not means-tested), and **individual** (paid to persons, not
households). Nearly every scheme labelled UBI in practice violates at least one condition, which
sharply limits what the resulting evidence can support.
