# CS 190 · Week 10 Discussion Questions
## The Future of Work: Automation, Jobs, Universal Basic Income

**Format:** 60-minute seminar. Come having done the prep assignment; the discussion assumes it.

---

## Part 1 — What the Evidence Says (≈15 minutes)

**1.** Frey and Osborne say 47%; Arntz, Gregory and Zierahn say 9%. **Neither is lying and neither
made an arithmetic error.** Explain to the room where the difference comes from. Which unit of
analysis — occupation or task — better describes the job you intend to hold?

**2.** Autor's ATM case is usually told as reassurance. Teller employment has declined since around
2010. **Does the later decline refute the argument, or illustrate it?** Defend your answer, and say
what it implies about the time horizon over which such reassurance is valid.

**3.** Acemoglu and Restrepo find effects that are statistically real, modest in aggregate, and
geographically concentrated. **Does "modest in aggregate but concentrated locally" make the problem
smaller or larger?** Smaller economically and larger politically is an easy answer — say what follows
from it.

**4.** Polanyi's paradox — we know more than we can tell — was the barrier that protected tacit
skill. Learned models do not need the rule stated. **Name one task you believe is still protected,
and say what would convince you it was not.**

---

## Part 2 — Universal Basic Income (≈20 minutes)

**5.** The lecture separated five arguments for UBI: automation insurance, administrative simplicity,
bargaining power, real freedom, and recognition of unpaid work. **Suppose the automation forecast
turns out to be entirely wrong and employment is fine in 2050. Which of the five survive?** What does
that tell you about how technologists usually argue for UBI?

**6.** A UBI funded by replacing existing targeted transfers can leave the most disadvantaged
recipients **worse off**, because a flat payment is smaller than the largest targeted ones. **Is a
programme that raises the floor for most while lowering it for the neediest an improvement?** Answer
with a principle, not a preference.

**7.** Finland found small positive employment effects and large wellbeing gains. **Which of those two
findings should carry more weight in a policy decision, and why?** Notice whether your answer depends
on believing that the point of the policy is employment.

**8.** The lecture claimed that a randomised trial **cannot** measure a UBI's most important effects,
because those are general-equilibrium effects that only appear when everyone has it permanently. If
that is right, what evidence *could* settle the question — and are we obliged to decide without it?

**9.** Both the political left and the political right have objected to UBI, for opposite reasons.
**Is that a point in its favour, a point against, or no evidence either way?**

---

## Part 3 — Obligation, and You (≈20 minutes)

**10.** You are asked to build a system that will do the work of forty people at your employer's
client. It is legal, technically interesting, and well paid. **What, if anything, are you required to
do beyond building it well?** "Nothing" is a permitted answer if defended.

**11.** The Luddites were skilled workers with a specific grievance about how a technology was
introduced, and they were substantially right about what would happen to them. They also lost.
**Does being right and losing generate any obligation in the winners?** Say to whom, and of what
kind.

**12.** Large language models write code. **State your position on obligations owed to displaced
workers in a form that still holds when the displaced worker is you.** If your position changes when
you are the subject, say why that is not simply self-interest.

---

## Closing (≈5 minutes)

Around the room, one sentence each:

> *"The claim about the future of work I am least confident in is ______, and I would change my mind
> if ______."*

**A prediction with no falsifier attached is a mood.** This is the last discussion before your
presentations; treat the sentence as practice for defending one.
