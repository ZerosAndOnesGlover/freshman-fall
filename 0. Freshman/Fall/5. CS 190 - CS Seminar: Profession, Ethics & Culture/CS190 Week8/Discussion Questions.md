# Discussion Questions — Week 8 Seminar
### Cybersecurity Ethics

**Come with written notes.** Participation is assessed on the quality of your reasoning, not the
confidence of your assertions.

---

## Part 1: The Disclosure Dilemma (~15 min)

**1.** You find a serious flaw in software used by roughly two million people. You report it to the
vendor, who acknowledges receipt and then goes silent for four months. What do you do, and what is the
strongest argument *against* whatever you chose?

**2.** Project Zero's 90-day deadline is deliberately fixed rather than negotiated. Is an arbitrary
deadline that vendors dislike better or worse than a flexible one they can extend indefinitely?
Defend a position.

**3.** You cannot know whether an attacker found the flaw before you did. How should that uncertainty
change your reasoning? Does it argue for faster disclosure, slower, or neither?

**4.** A vendor offers you $5,000 through its bug bounty. A broker offers $200,000 for the same
finding and will not say who the buyer is. Beyond the obvious, **what specifically makes the second
transaction different in kind** — and is your answer about the money, the secrecy, or the outcome?

---

## Part 2: Law Versus Ethics (~15 min)

**5.** *Van Buren* narrowed the CFAA but left the position of unsolicited testing unclear. Is it
defensible to test the security of a system you do not own if you sincerely intend to report what you
find? Separate your legal answer from your ethical one, and say whether they agree.

**6.** The DOJ's 2022 policy declines to prosecute good-faith security research. A colleague concludes
that research is now safe. What is wrong with that conclusion?

**7.** Consider a researcher who acts ethically and is prosecuted anyway. **Does the profession owe
them anything?** If so, what — and who is "the profession" in that sentence?

**8.** DMCA § 1201's security-research exemption must be renewed every three years and can be
narrowed or lost. What does it say about a legal regime that the right to do legitimate research is
temporary and requires periodic re-application?

---

## Part 3: Hacktivism (~15 min)

**9.** Classical civil disobedience is performed openly, and the participant accepts the penalty.
**Is anonymity fatal to the moral case for hacktivism**, or is it a reasonable adaptation to a state
with vastly greater surveillance capacity than in Thoreau's day?

**10.** A hacktivist group leaks documents proving a government agency broke the law. The same leak
exposes the personal data of 40,000 uninvolved citizens. How should that trade-off be assessed, and
by whom?

**11.** Every hacktivist believes their cause is just, including those whose causes you find
abhorrent. **Is there any principle that distinguishes them from the outside** — or does approval of
hacktivism reduce to approval of the particular cause?

**12.** Whistleblowing from inside authorised access and intrusion from outside are frequently
conflated in public debate. Does the distinction hold up under pressure? Construct a case where it is
genuinely difficult to say which one occurred.

---

## Closing

**One question to leave with:** you are three years into a job. You discover a flaw in your employer's
product that exposes customer data. Your manager tells you the fix is scheduled for the next release,
eight months away, and instructs you not to escalate.

**Write down now what you would do.** Keep it. You will be in a version of this meeting eventually,
and the reasoning you do today — with no career at stake — is more trustworthy than the reasoning you
will do in the room.
