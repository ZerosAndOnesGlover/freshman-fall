# Discussion Questions: Week 0 Seminar

These are the questions the seminar session will work through. You are not required to have polished answers — you are required to have *thought about them* before walking in. Bring notes.

---

## Part 1: Defining the Field (warm-up, ~10 min)

1. Before this week's reading, how would you have described "computer science" to a relative who isn't in tech? How has that description changed after the readings?
2. Dijkstra insists CS is "no more about computers than astronomy is about telescopes." Steelman the strongest objection to this analogy. (Hint: think about what's different between a telescope, which doesn't change what you're observing, and a computer, which arguably *does* shape the kinds of computational questions we even think to ask.)

## Part 2: CS vs. SE vs. CE (~15 min)

3. Pick one piece of code or one technical concept you've already encountered (in CS 101, PROG 101, or elsewhere) and classify it: is it primarily a CS question, an SE question, or a CE question? Justify your classification. (Example to react to, not to reuse: is "choosing whether to use a hash table or a sorted array for a given workload" a CS question or an SE question? Defend your answer.)
4. The lecture argues the boundaries between CS/SE/CE are "porous in practice." Can you think of a real engineering decision where getting the *category* wrong — treating an SE problem as if it were a pure CS problem, or vice versa — would lead to a bad outcome?

## Part 3: Careers and Self-Assessment (~10 min)

5. Looking at the career landscape in `Careers Reference Sheet.md`, which path are you currently most drawn to, and which course in your Year 1/Year 2 schedule do you expect to be most diagnostic for whether that's actually a good fit?
6. Is there a career path on that list you'd actively avoid? Why? (Be honest — there are no wrong answers here, but vague answers ("I don't like it") are weaker than specific ones ("I dislike X because Y").)

## Part 4: Why Ethics Belongs Here (~15 min, the core of the session)

7. Respond directly to the "technical neutrality is a myth" argument from the lecture. Can you think of a counterexample — a genuinely value-neutral technical artifact where the "neutrality is a myth" claim doesn't hold? Or do you find the claim fully persuasive?
8. The lecture claims engineers who think about failure modes and unintended consequences are *better engineers*, not just more ethical ones — i.e., that this seminar will make you technically stronger, not just more socially aware. Do you buy this claim? What would change your mind either way?
9. Civil engineers have PE licensure and a century of codified professional ethics partly because building collapses are visible and immediately fatal. Software failures are often invisible, diffuse, or delayed (a biased algorithm doesn't "collapse" the way a bridge does). Does this difference mean software engineering ethics should look *different* from civil engineering ethics, not just newer?

## Closing Question

10. Name one assumption about "being a computer scientist" that you walked in with today that this session has changed, complicated, or reinforced.
