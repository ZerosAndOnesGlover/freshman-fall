# Reading Guide: Week 0

This seminar is built on the assumption that you arrive at each session having done the reading. The readings below are short by design (this is a 1-credit seminar, not a fourth technical course) but they are meant to be read carefully, not skimmed.

---

## Required Reading

### 1. Dijkstra, E. — "On the Cruelty of Really Teaching Computing Science" (EWD1036, 1988)
**~10 pages. Free, widely available (search the title; it circulates as a plaintext memo).**

Dijkstra's famous (and famously combative) essay arguing that computer science is a mathematical discipline that has been historically miseducated as a craft. You don't need to agree with his conclusions, many practicing engineers don't, but you need to understand his argument, because it's the clearest articulation of the "CS is fundamentally mathematics" position you'll encounter.

**Guiding questions while reading:**
- What does Dijkstra mean when he says "the use of anthropomorphic terminology when dealing with computing systems is a symptom of professional immaturity"? Do you find this persuasive or pretentious?
- Dijkstra wrote this in 1988. Has the rise of machine learning (where systems do exhibit behavior that's hard to describe except in quasi-anthropomorphic terms) changed whether his argument still holds?

### 2. ACM: "Computer Science as a Profession" (ACM/IEEE Computing Curricula overview, relevant excerpt)
**~5 pages. Free at acm.org or via the ACM/IEEE Computing Curricula reports.**

A more institutional, consensus-driven account of how the field defines itself, written by the bodies that actually accredit CS programs. Useful as a counterweight to Dijkstra's individual (and idiosyncratic) take.

**Guiding question:**
- Where does this document's definition of "computer science" diverge from Dijkstra's? Where does it converge?

### 3. Excerpt: Russell & Norvig, *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach*, 4th ed., Section 1.1 ("What is AI?")
**~6 pages.**

You won't take AI formally until CS 331 in Year 3, but this short excerpt is useful here because it models exactly the kind of "what *is* this field, actually" reasoning this week asks you to do, applied to a sub-field instead of the whole discipline. Notice the structure of their argument as much as the content.

## Optional / Enrichment Reading

### 4. "What Color is Your Function?": Bob Nystrom (blog post, free online)
A short, well-written piece that's nominally about async/await semantics in programming languages, but is included here as an example of *excellent technical writing about a hard idea* — the kind of clarity this seminar will ask of your own position papers later in the semester.

### 5. ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct (full document, 2018 revision)
We will read this formally in Week 3, but if you want to get ahead, it's short (under 10 pages) and free at acm.org/code-of-ethics. No assignment attached to it this week, just useful context.

## A Note on How to Read for This Seminar

Technical reading (a textbook chapter, documentation) is usually read to *extract information*. Seminar reading should be read differently, to *extract and evaluate an argument*. For each required reading this week, you should be able to answer, in one or two sentences each:

1. What is the author's central claim?
2. What is the strongest piece of evidence or reasoning they offer for it?
3. What is the strongest counterargument someone could raise?
4. Where do you personally land, and why?

Bring written notes (even rough ones) answering these four questions for each required reading. This is the basis of your participation grade this week.
