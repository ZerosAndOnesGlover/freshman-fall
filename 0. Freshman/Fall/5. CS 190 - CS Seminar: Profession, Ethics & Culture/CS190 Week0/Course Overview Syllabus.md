## Course Description

CS 190 introduces the non-technical dimensions of a computing career: what the profession looks like,
how to navigate it, and the ethical responsibilities it entails. Computation increasingly shapes
society — in surveillance, hiring, criminal justice, healthcare, and political processes. Engineers
who build these systems bear responsibility for their effects. **This seminar develops the vocabulary
and moral reasoning to discharge that responsibility thoughtfully.**

It is a one-credit seminar, and it is not a soft option. There is no examination, but there are three
written position papers, a presentation, and weekly preparation that the discussion assumes you have
done. **The course is graded on the quality of your reasoning, never on the content of your
conclusions.**

---

## What This Course Is Not

It is not a compliance course. You will not be given a list of prohibited actions to memorise.

It is not a course in which the instructor's views are the correct answers. Every assignment in this
course has been marked full credit for positions the instructor disagrees with, and will continue to
be.

It is not a course about other people's failures. The cases are chosen because they are hard, not
because the engineers involved were negligent — and most of them were not.

### One Documented Deviation

The curriculum lists **"Open Source Licenses"** under Week 7 (Intellectual Property). **The licence
taxonomy is taught in Week 2 instead**, alongside the open source ecosystem it belongs to —
permissive versus copyleft, MIT/BSD/Apache 2.0 against GPL/LGPL/AGPL, licence compatibility, and
contributor licence agreements.

Nothing is dropped: the union of Weeks 2 and 7 covers the union of the two topics as listed. The
reason for the placement is that **Week 2 explains what the licences say and Week 7 explains why they
bind.** A licence is an exercise of the copyright an author automatically holds, so the mechanics are
only intelligible once the underlying right is — which is why Week 7 returns to copyleft violation
and classifies it as copyright infringement rather than a breach of etiquette.

Every other week follows the curriculum topic list exactly.

### The Position Papers Escalate Deliberately

This is the architecture of the course, and it is worth knowing in advance.

- **Paper #1 (Week 3)** gives you an authoritative code — the ACM Code of Ethics — and asks you to
  apply it to a conflict between employer instruction and public interest. **The scaffold is
  provided.**
- **Paper #2 (Week 6)** removes the code. It asks who is responsible when a technology functioning
  *exactly as designed* causes harm, which requires you to **construct** a framework for assigning
  responsibility rather than consulting one.
- **Paper #3 (Week 9)** removes the identifiable agent. It asks what an individual owes regarding a
  harm that **nobody decided** — one emerging from many uncoordinated, individually defensible
  choices.

Most ethical frameworks were built to evaluate actions by agents, and handle the third case badly.
**Discovering where your framework runs out is the point**, not a failure of the assignment. Paper #3
has an extended two-week window for this reason, and because Week 10's material on automation bears
directly on it.

All three papers are **750–1,000 words**, hard limits. Papers outside the range are returned ungraded.

### Seminar Preparation

A prep assignment is set for each of Weeks 0–10 (eleven in total) and is due **before** the seminar it
belongs to. They run 250–400 words and are graded on completion and seriousness rather than on
correctness. **The discussion assumes you have done them**, which is the real reason they exist.

The lowest is dropped, without a reason being required. Weeks 11 and 12 substitute the presentation
and the question preparation respectively.

### Seminar Contribution

Assessed by the instructor across the term. **Contribution is not volume.** A student who speaks
twice in an hour, to the point, having listened, scores above one who speaks eight times without
engaging what anyone else said. Restating someone's position accurately before disagreeing with it
counts as contribution of the highest kind.

If speaking in a group is difficult for you, tell the instructor in Week 0 or 1. Arrangements exist
and do not require you to explain why.

## Ground Rules

Established in Week 3 and restated in Weeks 9 and 11:

> **Arguments are about evidence and reasoning. No argument may require anyone present to defend
> their own existence in the room as a debating position.**

Some sessions — Week 9 in particular — concern conditions that people in the room are living under.
Presenters and discussants may be arguing from experience. **Disagree with the argument, which is
what has been offered for disagreement.**

Two corollaries: do not name what someone's argument implies about their own circumstances if they
did not state it, and do not treat a person's presence as evidence for or against a claim.

## Reading

There is **no required textbook.** Readings are assigned weekly — typically 40–70 pages — and are
primary sources wherever possible: the ACM Code itself, the GDPR text, court opinions, the original
research papers behind the headline numbers.

This is deliberate. A great deal of what circulates about these topics is second-hand summary of a
finding, and the summaries are frequently wrong in ways that change the conclusion. **Week 10 exists
partly to demonstrate exactly that**, using a statistic you have probably already repeated.

Optional readings are genuinely optional and are marked as such.

## Academic Integrity

The standard university policy applies. Two clarifications specific to this course:

**On sources.** Cite what you used. A position paper that reproduces an argument from a reading
without attribution is plagiarism even where the wording differs, because in this course **the
argument is the work.**

**On generative tools.** You may use them to check grammar or to find sources, and you must say so.
You may not submit an argument you did not construct. The reason is not that it is against the rules;
it is that **the entire value of this course is the practice of reasoning under pressure**, and a
paper you did not reason your way to has cost you the credit while giving you nothing. Week 6 and
Week 10 discuss these tools at length; you will be in a better position to use them well afterwards.

## Accessibility and Support

Anything you need in order to participate — presenting seated, extended time, materials in advance,
a quieter room, an alternative to speaking in the full group — is arranged by emailing the
instructor, **without requiring you to disclose a reason.**

This is stated on the syllabus rather than buried in a handbook because **Week 9 spends an hour on
the cost of environments in which people do not ask.** It would be an odd course that discussed that
and then made asking difficult.

## What You Should Be Able to Do by Week 12

1. Locate the ethically contested question in a technical proposal, and state it precisely.
2. Apply the ACM Code, and say where it stops being decisive.
3. Explain how a system with no biased decision anywhere in it produces disparate outcomes.
4. Distinguish informed consent from purpose limitation, and say why regulators moved toward the latter.
5. Assign responsibility for a harm produced by a system working exactly as designed.
6. Reason about a harm with no identifiable decider — and say honestly where your framework runs out.
7. Read a contested empirical literature without collapsing it into a side.
8. State an opposing view in the form its proponent would accept.
9. Make an argument in five minutes, aloud, and concede what you cannot answer.
