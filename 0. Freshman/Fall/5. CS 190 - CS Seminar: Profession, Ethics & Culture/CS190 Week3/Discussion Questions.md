# Discussion Questions: Week 3 Seminar

---

## Part 1: The Code Itself (~15 min)

1. The ACM Code opens with the obligation to "contribute to society and to human well-being, acknowledging that all people are stakeholders in computing." Most engineers work for private companies whose primary obligation is to shareholders and customers, not to "all people." Is principle 1.1 a genuine professional obligation or an aspirational statement that no one is actually held to? What would it mean to take it seriously in a commercial context?

2. Principle 1.2 requires avoiding "unnecessary" harm. Consider: a company builds a credit scoring algorithm that denies loans to some applicants who would have repaid them. Those false negatives are a real harm. The company's position is that the harm is "necessary" because a less selective algorithm would accept too many true defaults, threatening the company's financial viability. Walk through who has the legitimate standing to determine whether this harm is "unnecessary," and what process would resolve the disagreement.

3. The code prohibits discrimination based on a list of protected characteristics including "any other unjustified characteristic." How do you distinguish a justified basis for differential treatment from an unjustified one? Give an example of an algorithm that treats people differently in a justified way and one that treats people differently in an unjustified way. What principle distinguishes them?

## Part 2: The Three Scenarios (~20 min)

Work through each scenario from the lecture. For each: identify which ACM principles apply, state what the code appears to require, and then separately state what you think you would actually do and whether those two answers differ.

4. **Scenario A (Facial Recognition):** You know the system has a 34% error rate for dark-skinned women. It ships in two weeks. The contract is critical. What do you do? Does "I documented my objection in writing and was overruled" satisfy your professional obligations under the code, or does it fall short?

5. **Scenario B (Hospital Vulnerability):** The code's principle 1.2 says harm must be reported to "a responsible party." Who is the responsible party here — your manager, the hospital's IT security team, a government health regulator, the public? Does the answer change if the "responsible party" you report to refuses to act?

6. **Scenario C (Recommendation Algorithm):** You didn't build the configuration causing harm. You didn't know about this deployment. You discovered it incidentally. Principle 2.5 says to "give comprehensive and thorough evaluations of computer systems and their impacts, with special emphasis on possible risks." Does this principle assign you an obligation to investigate systems you didn't build, if you work at the company that operates them?

## Part 3: Voluntary vs. Mandatory Accountability (~10 min)

7. The strongest argument for mandatory computing licensure is that voluntary codes lack enforcement mechanisms. The strongest argument against is that the field changes too fast for licensure to remain meaningful. Which argument do you find more persuasive, and why? Is there a middle-ground mechanism — not full PE-style licensure, but more than a voluntary code — that you think would be effective?

8. Shannon Vallor's virtue ethics approach asks: "what kind of engineer does building this system make you?" Apply this question to one of the three scenarios above. Does it generate a different answer than the ACM Code's rule-based approach? Which answer is more action-guiding?

## Closing

9. Position Papers begin this week. Looking at the three scenarios, which one do you think generates the most genuinely difficult ethical question — not the one you feel most strongly about, but the one where thoughtful people who've read the code carefully could reach different conclusions? Why is it hard?
