# Glossary: Week 3 Additions

---

**Code of Ethics**
A codified set of professional obligations and conduct standards adopted by a professional body, intended to guide practitioners in their work, provide a basis for accountability, and establish shared standards for public trust. Distinguished from personal ethics by being institutional and shared rather than individual. The ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct (2018) is the primary code for computing professionals.

**Professional Licensure**
A legally enforced credential system in which practitioners must demonstrate minimum competence standards and agree to professional conduct obligations before being permitted to practice. In engineering: the PE (Professional Engineer) license in the US, requiring accredited education, supervised experience, and passage of the Principles and Practice of Engineering exam. Computing lacks mandatory licensure in most jurisdictions — the ACM Code is voluntary.

**Public Interest**
The aggregate well-being of all people who may be affected by a decision or system, as distinguished from the interest of a specific client, employer, or user group. The ACM Code establishes the public interest as the primary obligation of a computing professional — superseding employer or client loyalty when they conflict.

**Foreseeable Harm**
Harm that a reasonably competent professional in the relevant domain could have anticipated as a probable or plausible consequence of a design decision or deployment choice. The professional obligation to avoid harm applies to foreseeable harm, not to harm that was genuinely unforeseeable. Determining what was "foreseeable" is often contested after the fact.

**Diffusion of Responsibility**
A social psychological phenomenon (also studied as an organizational ethics problem) in which the perceived responsibility of each individual in a group decreases as the size of the group increases. In large software organizations, each individual engineer's contribution to a harmful system is small enough that no individual feels fully responsible for the outcome. The aggregate result is that harmful systems ship without any individual having made a conscious decision to cause harm.

**Whistleblowing**
The act of disclosing, to an external party (regulator, press, public), information about organizational wrongdoing that the organization wishes to keep internal. Distinguished from internal reporting (raising concerns within the organization). Computing-relevant whistleblowing examples: Edward Snowden's disclosure of NSA surveillance programs (2013); Frances Haugen's disclosure of Facebook internal research on platform harms (2021). Carries significant personal and legal risk for the whistleblower. Protected in varying degrees by law in different jurisdictions.

**Confidentiality (Professional)**
The obligation to protect information acquired in the course of professional work from disclosure to unauthorized parties. A real and significant professional obligation — but one the ACM Code explicitly limits: confidentiality does not apply when keeping a secret "would endanger health or safety, acts unethically, or is contrary to law." The tension between confidentiality obligations and obligations to the public is one of the most common ethical conflicts computing professionals face.

**Steelmanning**
The practice of constructing the strongest possible version of an opposing argument before responding to it — the opposite of strawmanning (attacking a weak or distorted version of the opposing view). A methodological discipline for rigorous argumentation: you have not seriously engaged with an objection until you have stated it in the form its most thoughtful proponent would recognize as their own.

**Consequentialism**
An ethical framework holding that the moral status of an action is determined entirely by its consequences — specifically, by whether it maximizes some measure of good outcomes (utility, welfare, happiness). In applied ethics: "should I ship this system?" becomes "what are the probable outcomes, how good or bad are they, and how do the probabilities weight them?" Criticized for difficulty of predicting consequences and for potentially licensing harmful actions when they produce net positive outcomes.

**Deontology**
An ethical framework holding that the moral status of an action is determined by whether it conforms to moral rules or duties — independent of consequences. Kant's categorical imperative is the canonical formulation. In applied ethics: "should I ship this system?" becomes "would a universal rule permitting this be rationally defensible?" Criticized for rigidity when rules conflict and for ignoring outcomes that seem obviously morally relevant.

**Virtue Ethics**
An ethical framework centered on character rather than rules or consequences — asking what a person of good character would do, or what habits of character a given action cultivates or erodes. Shannon Vallor applies this to technology ethics: "what kind of engineer does building this system make you?" Criticized for vagueness about what virtues are relevant and for providing less action-guidance than rule-based frameworks.
