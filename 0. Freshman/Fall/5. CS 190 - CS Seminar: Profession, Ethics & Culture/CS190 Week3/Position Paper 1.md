## The Prompt

The ACM Code of Ethics states that computing professionals have an obligation to the public interest that supersedes their obligations to employers and clients when they conflict. Principle 1.1 identifies "all people" as stakeholders; principle 2.9 explicitly states that a professional should "support members of the public if they come in conflict with the professional's employer or client, particularly where they concern violations of privacy, copyright, proprietary information, or concerns about the public safety."

However, most computing professionals are employees. They work under employment contracts that include confidentiality obligations, often non-disparagement clauses, and in some jurisdictions non-compete agreements. They face real economic consequences for refusing to do what their employer asks or for disclosing employer conduct publicly. The ACM Code does not pay their rent.

**Your task:** Take and defend a position on the following question:

*When an engineer believes that a system they are being asked to build or maintain poses a meaningful risk of harm to the public, what are their professional obligations — and how far do those obligations extend?*

Your paper must do all of the following:

1. **State a clear position** in your opening paragraph. Do not hedge into "on the one hand / on the other hand" and never commit. Take a stance.
2. **Engage with the ACM Code specifically** — not just with the concept of professional ethics generally. Quote and analyze at least two specific principles.
3. **Address the strongest counterargument to your position** and explain why your position survives it. A paper that ignores the best case against it is a weak paper.
4. **Ground your argument in at least one concrete case** — one of the three scenarios from the lecture, a real historical case, or a hypothetical you construct. Abstract arguments about professional ethics that never touch ground are unconvincing.
5. **Reach a conclusion that has practical implications** — your reader should know, after reading your paper, what an engineer in this situation should actually do.

## What a Strong Paper Looks Like

A strong paper takes a specific, falsifiable position. "Engineers have a duty to avoid serious harm" is not a position — it is a truism. "Engineers who have documented evidence of a public safety risk and have raised it internally without resolution are obligated to disclose externally, even at personal professional cost" is a position.

A strong paper steelmans the counterargument. If your position is that external disclosure is obligatory, the strongest counterargument is not "but your employer will fire you" (the code does not say professional obligations are waived when compliance is costly). The strongest counterargument is something like: "premature external disclosure of an unverified risk could itself cause harm — panic, exploitation by bad actors, regulatory overreaction — that outweighs the harm the disclosure was meant to prevent." Engage with that version, not the weak one.

A strong paper does not moralize. You are making an argument, not expressing disapproval. "Companies that cause harm are bad" is not an argument. "Companies that knowingly ship systems with known, significant, measurable risks of public harm, having received documented internal objections, have violated the spirit of ACM principle 1.2 and created conditions under which external disclosure is justified" is an argument.

## A Note on AI Writing Assistance

You may use AI tools (including large language models) to brainstorm, outline, or identify counterarguments. You may not use them to generate the text of your paper. Your position paper is a demonstration of *your* reasoning and *your* judgment — the whole point is to practice taking and defending a position yourself.
