## Course Description

CS 101 is your entry point into the science of computation. Not just programming: but understanding what computation *is*, what it *can* do, and what it fundamentally *cannot* do.

We start at the most basic question: what does it mean for something to be computable? From there, we build a precise vocabulary for describing algorithms, analyzing their efficiency, and implementing them in Python.

This course emphasizes **thinking over typing**. You will learn to model problems abstractly before writing a single line of code. You will learn to read and reason about code as rigorously as a mathematician reads a proof. By the end, you will be able to take a problem you have never seen, decompose it, design a solution, implement it correctly, and analyze how it scales.

## Required Textbooks

### Primary Texts

**1. Guttag, J. — Introduction to Computation and Programming Using Python, 3rd ed.**
*(MIT Press, 2021)*
The primary Python text. Accessible but rigorous. We cover Chapters 1–12.

**2. Cormen, T., Leiserson, C., Rivest, R., & Stein, C. — Introduction to Algorithms, 4th ed. (CLRS)**
*(MIT Press, 2022)*
The definitive algorithms reference. We use Chapters 1–4 in this course. You will return to this book for the next four years.

### Reference Texts

**3. Sipser, M. — Introduction to the Theory of Computation, 3rd ed.**
*(Cengage, 2012)*
For computability foundations (Weeks 11–12). This book recurs in CS 301.

**4. Knuth, D. — The Art of Computer Programming, Vol. 1: Fundamental Algorithms**
Optional. Legendary. Read it for intellectual enrichment. No specific assignments — but students who read Knuth think differently.

**5. Python 3 Official Documentation**
https://docs.python.org/3/
Always open in a browser tab when coding.

> **A note on these weights.** The Year 1 curriculum document gives this course's assessment as
> *Problem Sets 40%, Midterms 30%, Final 20%, Lab 10%*. Midterms, Final and Lab match exactly. The
> **40% for problem sets is split here into Problem Sets 30% and Projects 10%**, because the
> curriculum document's own weekly schedule assigns two projects — a data analysis tool and, in
> Week 12, final project presentations — and work of that size assessed under the problem-set line
> would make a single project worth more than several problem sets combined without saying so.
>
> The graded total is unchanged at 100%, and the union of problem sets and projects carries the 40%
> the curriculum document specifies. If the curriculum document is later revised to weight projects
> explicitly, that revision governs.

> **Quizzes carry no direct weight.** The 11 Wednesday quizzes are **formative**: they are marked and
> returned so you and the staff can see where you stand, but they do not enter the course grade.
> Each quiz covers the *previous* week's material, which makes them the cheapest possible early
> warning before that material appears on a midterm or the final. Treat them as free diagnostics.

## Collaboration Policy

**Problem Sets:** You may discuss problems with classmates, but **all written/coded work must be your own**. You must understand everything you submit. If you can't explain it to a TA, you haven't learned it.

**Labs:** Collaboration is encouraged. You may work with a partner but both must submit individually.

**Exams:** No collaboration. No notes except the allowed cheat sheet.

**Projects:** Individual unless specified otherwise.

**AI Tools (ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot, etc.):** Using AI to generate code you submit as your own is academic dishonesty. You may use AI to *explain* concepts you don't understand. The goal of this course is to develop your thinking — AI cannot do that for you.

## Academic Integrity

Violations of academic integrity will be reported to the academic integrity office. Penalties range from a zero on the assignment to course failure.

**What counts as a violation:**
- Submitting another student's work as your own
- Copying code from the internet without attribution
- Using AI tools to generate submitted work
- Sharing your solutions with other students

**What does NOT count as a violation:**
- Discussing problem approaches at a high level
- Using course resources (lecture notes, textbooks)
- Getting help from office hours
- Looking up Python documentation

When in doubt, ask.

## Why This Course Is Designed This Way

You may wonder why CS 101 covers theory (Turing Machines, computability, Big-O) rather than just teaching you to code. Here is the honest answer:

**Python syntax can be learned in a week from YouTube.** What takes years to develop is the ability to *think algorithmically* — to see the mathematical structure of a problem, design a correct and efficient solution, and reason about why it works.

That is what this course builds. The syntax is a vehicle. The thinking is the destination.

Employers and graduate schools care that you can solve problems they haven't shown you before. That requires deep understanding, not pattern-matching. Every hard concept in this course is hard because it is rich with content — not because it is obscure. The difficulty is the point.
