# PHYS 141 · Problem Set 4
## Work, Energy & Conservation of Energy

**Released:** Friday, Week 4 | **Due:** Friday, Week 5, 11:59 PM
**Instructions:** State clearly whether you are using the work-energy theorem or conservation of mechanical energy for each problem. Show all steps. Report answers with correct units and significant figures.
**Points:** 5 per problem · **100 total**

### Part A: Work (Problems 1–5) — 25 pts

**1.** A 20 kg crate is pushed 12 m across a horizontal floor by a horizontal force of 85 N, while friction of 30 N opposes the motion.

(a) Find the work done by the applied force.
(b) Find the work done by friction.
(c) Find the work done by gravity and by the normal force.
(d) Find the net work done on the crate.

---

**2.** A force F⃗ = (6, −3) N acts on an object as it moves through displacement d⃗ = (4, 5) m.

(a) Compute the work done using the dot product formula.
(b) Compute the magnitude of F⃗ and d⃗, and the angle between them. Verify W = Fd cosθ gives the same answer as part (a).

---

**3.** A variable force acting on an object is given by F(x) = 5x² (in Newtons, x in meters), directed along the x-axis. Find the work done as the object moves from x = 1 m to x = 4 m.

---

**4.** A 1200 kg elevator moves upward at constant velocity for 15 m.

(a) Find the work done by the tension in the cable.
(b) Find the work done by gravity.
(c) Find the net work done on the elevator. Explain why this must be zero given the described motion.

---

**5.** A pendulum bob swings from a string. At the lowest point of its swing, the string tension does zero work — but at other points along the arc, doesn't the string exert a force with a component in the direction of motion? Explain, using the definition of work, why the tension does zero work at every point of the swing, not just the bottom.

---

### Part B: Work-Energy Theorem (Problems 6–10) — 25 pts

**6.** A 1500 kg car traveling at 25 m/s brakes to a stop over a distance of 60 m.

(a) Use the work-energy theorem to find the braking force (assumed constant).
(b) Find the coefficient of kinetic friction between the tires and road that would produce this braking force (assume the brakes lock the wheels, so kinetic friction acts).

---

**7.** A 0.15 kg baseball is thrown at 40 m/s. It is caught by a fielder whose glove decelerates it to rest over a distance of 0.12 m.

(a) Use the work-energy theorem to find the average force exerted by the glove on the ball.
(b) Compare this force to the ball's weight. Comment on why catching a fast ball "stings."

---

**8.** A 3.0 kg block starts at rest and is acted on by a net force F(x) = 12 − 2x (Newtons, x in meters, valid for 0 ≤ x ≤ 6 m).

(a) Find the work done by this force as the block moves from x=0 to x=6 m (integrate).
(b) Use the work-energy theorem to find the speed at x = 6 m.
(c) At what position x is the block's speed maximum? (Hint: speed is maximum when KE is maximum, which occurs where the net force changes sign — i.e., where cumulative work stops increasing.)

---

**9.** A 5.0 kg object slides across a rough horizontal floor. It starts at 8.0 m/s and comes to rest after sliding 10 m.

(a) Use the work-energy theorem to find the friction force.
(b) Find the coefficient of kinetic friction.
(c) If the object's initial speed were doubled (16 m/s), how far would it slide before stopping? (Do not assume — derive this from the work-energy theorem and compare to part (a)'s stopping distance.)

---

**10.** A rocket of mass 500 kg (approximately constant over this short interval) experiences a net force that does work W(t)... Actually — this problem requires position, not time. A 500 kg rocket sled starts at rest and experiences a net force that varies as F(x) = 8000 − 400x (N, x in meters) for 0 ≤ x ≤ 20 m.

(a) At what position does the force become zero?
(b) Find the total work done from x=0 to x=20 m.
(c) Find the speed at x=20 m using the work-energy theorem.
(d) Find the speed at the position where F=0 (from part a). Is this the maximum speed reached over the interval 0 to 20 m? Explain.

### Part C: Potential Energy & Conservative Forces (Problems 11–14) — 20 pts

**11.** A 2.0 kg object has potential energy U(x) = 4x³ − 18x² + 6 (Joules, x in meters).

(a) Find the force F(x).
(b) Find all equilibrium positions (where F = 0).
(c) For each equilibrium position, determine if it is stable or unstable using the second derivative test.

---

**12.** A spring with k = 250 N/m is compressed 0.20 m from its natural length.

(a) Find the elastic potential energy stored.
(b) If this spring launches a 0.40 kg block horizontally (frictionless surface), find the launch speed.
(c) If the same spring instead launches the block up a frictionless 20° incline, find how far up the incline the block travels before momentarily stopping.

---

**13.** Show, using the path-independence definition, that a force F⃗ = (3y, 2x) N is NOT conservative. (Hint: compute work done by this force along two different paths between the same two points — e.g., (0,0) to (2,2) via a straight line, and via the path (0,0)→(2,0)→(2,2) — and show the results differ.)

---

**14.** A 1.5 kg object is released from rest at the top of a frictionless curved ramp of height 3.0 m. The ramp shape is arbitrary (not necessarily a straight incline).

(a) Using conservation of energy, find the speed at the bottom of the ramp.
(b) Does your answer depend on the specific shape of the ramp? Justify using the conservative-force property of gravity.
(c) If a small friction force were present, would the answer to (b) still hold? Explain why or why not.

---

### Part D: Conservation of Energy (Problems 15–20) — 30 pts

**15.** A 4.0 kg block slides down a frictionless 30° incline, starting from rest at a height of 5.0 m (measured vertically).

(a) Find the speed at the bottom using energy conservation.
(b) Find the speed at the bottom using kinematics (a = g sinθ from Week 3) and compare.
(c) Does the length of the incline matter for the final speed? Why or why not?

---

**16.** A roller coaster car (mass 800 kg) starts from rest at the top of a 45 m hill. Ignoring friction, find its speed at the bottom of the hill, and then its speed at the top of a second hill of height 20 m (assuming it doesn't leave the track).

---

**17.** A 0.20 kg ball is thrown from ground level at 15 m/s at 50° above horizontal.

(a) Use energy conservation to find the maximum height reached. (Careful: at maximum height, only the vertical component of velocity is zero — the horizontal component remains! Use KE at the top with only the horizontal velocity component.)
(b) Verify your answer using the projectile motion formula H = (v₀sinθ)²/(2g) from Week 2.

---

**18. (Loop-the-loop, extended)** A 0.50 kg ball starts at height h above the bottom of a frictionless circular loop of radius 0.80 m.

(a) Find the minimum h for the ball to complete the loop (use the derivation method from Lecture 15).
(b) If h = 3.0 m (greater than the minimum), find the ball's speed at the top of the loop.
(c) Find the normal force on the ball at the top of the loop when h = 3.0 m. (You'll need F=ma for the centripetal direction, combined with your energy-derived speed.)

---

**19. (Friction + energy, synthesis)** A 6.0 kg block starts at rest at the top of a 4.0 m long, 35° incline (μ_k = 0.15), then slides onto a horizontal frictionless surface at the bottom, then compresses a spring (k = 500 N/m) that brings it to rest.

(a) Find the block's speed at the bottom of the incline using the energy equation with friction work.
(b) Since the horizontal surface is frictionless, find the speed just before hitting the spring (should be unchanged from part a).
(c) Find the maximum compression of the spring.
(d) If the horizontal surface instead had friction (μ_k = 0.10) over a distance of 2.0 m before the spring, how would your answer to (c) change? Set up but do not necessarily complete every arithmetic step — show the modified energy equation.

---

**20. (Power synthesis)** A 70 kg cyclist rides up a 6° incline at a constant speed of 8.0 m/s. Ignore air resistance; assume rolling friction provides a resisting force of 15 N.

(a) Find the force the cyclist must exert (through the pedals, ultimately via the tires) to maintain constant speed up the incline.
(b) Find the power output required.
(c) Convert this power to horsepower.
(d) If the cyclist can only sustain 300 W, what is the maximum incline angle they can climb at 8.0 m/s (ignoring rolling friction for this part, for simplicity)?
