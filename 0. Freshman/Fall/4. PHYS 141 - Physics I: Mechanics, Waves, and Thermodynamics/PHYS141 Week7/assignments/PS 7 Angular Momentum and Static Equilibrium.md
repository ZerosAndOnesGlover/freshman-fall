### Part A: Angular Momentum (Problems 1–6) — 30 pts

**1.** A 0.30 kg particle moves in a circle of radius 1.5 m at 6.0 m/s. Find its angular momentum about the center of the circle.

---

**2.** A 1.0 kg particle moves in a straight line along the path y=4.0 m (parallel to the x-axis) at constant velocity 3.0 m/s in the +x direction.

(a) Find its angular momentum about the origin (0,0).
(b) Find its angular momentum about the point (0, 4.0) — a point ON its line of motion.
(c) Explain the difference between your answers to (a) and (b) using the geometric definition of angular momentum (L=mvr sinφ, where r is measured to the particle and φ is the angle to its velocity — or more simply, using the perpendicular distance from the point to the line of motion).

---

**3.** A solid disk (M=5.0 kg, R=0.40 m) spins at ω=10 rad/s. Find its angular momentum.

---

**4.** A rotating system's angular momentum changes from 8.0 kg·m²/s to 20.0 kg·m²/s over 4.0 s due to a constant applied torque. Find the torque.

---

**5.** A 2.0 kg particle at position (3.0, 0, 0) m has velocity (0, 5.0, 0) m/s.

(a) Find its angular momentum vector about the origin.
(b) A force F⃗=(0,0,10) N (in the z-direction) acts on it. Find the torque about the origin due to this force, and verify it is consistent with how L would need to change (conceptually — you don't need to integrate, just check the direction makes sense given τ=dL/dt).

---

**6.** A merry-go-round (I=400 kg·m²) spins at ω=1.2 rad/s with no external torque acting. Its angular momentum is measured over time and found to be exactly constant at 480 kg·m²/s throughout a 20 s interval, even though 3 children jump on at various points during this time. Explain, using the concept of "isolated system" and internal vs. external torques, why this is expected regardless of how or where the children land (assuming they land with no horizontal velocity relative to the ground, i.e., no initial angular momentum of their own about the axis).

### Part B: Conservation of Angular Momentum (Problems 7–13) — 35 pts

**7.** A skater spins at 3.0 rad/s with arms extended, $I_i=5.0$ kg·m². Pulling arms in reduces I to 2.0 kg·m².

(a) Find the new angular velocity.
(b) Find the kinetic energy before and after. Where does the energy change come from?

---

**8.** A diver has $I_i=12$ kg·m² and $\omega_i=1.8$ rad/s leaving the board. Tucking reduces I to 3.0 kg·m² for 1.5 complete somersaults.

(a) Find ω during the tuck.
(b) Find the time spent in the tuck to complete 1.5 revolutions (assume constant ω during the tuck itself).
(c) If the diver then extends back out to $I=12$ kg·m² before entering the water, find the final ω.

---

**9.** A 60 kg person stands at the center of a rotating platform (I_platform=200 kg·m², person's I≈0 at center) spinning at 2.0 rad/s. The person walks out to r=1.5 m from the center.

(a) Find the new angular velocity (treat the person as a point mass once at radius r).
(b) Find the fractional decrease in angular velocity.

---

**10.** A uniform rod (M=2.0 kg, L=1.2 m, $I=\frac{1}{12}ML^2$ about its center) spins about its center at ω₀=4.0 rad/s on a frictionless horizontal surface. Two 0.50 kg point masses, initially at the rod's center (contributing no I), slide outward along the rod (frictionless slots) to the very ends of the rod. Find the new angular velocity.

---

**11.** A disk (I₁=0.60 kg·m²) spins at ω₀=12 rad/s. A second, identical but stationary disk (I₂=0.60 kg·m²) is dropped onto it and they stick together (same axis).

(a) Find the common final angular velocity.
(b) Find the kinetic energy lost in this rotational "collision."
(c) Compare this problem's structure to a linear perfectly-inelastic collision between two equal masses, one initially at rest. What is the analogous fractional KE loss in the linear case (from Week 5), and does it match your answer here?

---

**12. (Kepler's Second Law application)** A comet at its closest approach to the Sun (perihelion, r=0.5 AU) moves at 60 km/s. Find its speed at its farthest point (aphelion, r=35 AU), using conservation of angular momentum. (AU = astronomical unit; you don't need to convert to meters — the ratio of radii is what matters, since L=mvr is conserved and m cancels.)

---

**13.** A star of radius $R_0=7\times10^8$ m (roughly the Sun's radius) rotates once every 25 days. It collapses (supernova) to a neutron star of radius $R_f=12$ km, conserving both mass and angular momentum, with moment of inertia $I=\frac{2}{5}MR^2$ (uniform sphere) before and after.

(a) Find the ratio $I_f/I_i$ in terms of $(R_f/R_0)^2$.
(b) Find the new rotation period, using $I_i\omega_i=I_f\omega_f$ and $\omega=2\pi/T$.
(c) Comment on whether this rotation rate is physically plausible for a real neutron star (compare to observed millisecond pulsars, which rotate hundreds of times per second).

### Part C: Static Equilibrium (Problems 14–20) — 35 pts

**14.** A uniform beam of length 8.0 m and weight 600 N is supported at both ends. A 900 N weight is placed 3.0 m from the left end. Find the force exerted by each support.

---

**15.** A 4.0 m uniform ladder weighing 180 N leans against a frictionless wall at 65° from the horizontal. Find the normal force from the wall, the normal force from the floor, and the minimum coefficient of static friction at the floor to prevent slipping.

---

**16.** A 70 kg person stands on the ladder from Problem 15, at a point 2.5 m up the ladder (measured along its length from the base). Find the new normal force from the wall and the new minimum coefficient of static friction required. (Add the person's weight as an additional force at the specified point, in addition to the ladder's own weight at its midpoint.)

---

**17.** A horizontal uniform beam of length 5.0 m and weight 300 N is hinged to a wall at one end. A cable from the far end makes a 35° angle with the beam and is anchored to the wall above the hinge. No additional load hangs from the beam.

(a) Find the tension in the cable.
(b) Find the horizontal and vertical components of the hinge force.
(c) Find the magnitude of the total hinge force.

---

**18.** A shop sign of weight 120 N hangs from the end of a horizontal uniform bracket of length 0.80 m and weight 40 N, which is hinged to a wall at one end. A support cable runs from the far end of the bracket to the wall at 50° above the bracket.

(a) Find the cable tension.
(b) Find the horizontal and vertical hinge force components.

---

**19. (Synthesis — see-saw with three forces)** A uniform see-saw (length 4.0 m, weight 250 N, pivoted at its exact center) has a 300 N weight placed 1.5 m from the left end and an unknown weight W placed 1.0 m from the right end. Find W such that the see-saw is balanced (in rotational equilibrium). (The pivot itself provides whatever normal force is needed for translational equilibrium — you don't need to find it for this problem, just use the torque balance about the center pivot.)

---

**20. (Comprehensive synthesis — equilibrium + friction)** A uniform ladder of length 5.0 m and weight 200 N leans against a rough wall (NOT frictionless this time — the wall now also provides friction $f_w$, vertical, in addition to its normal force) at angle 55° from horizontal. The floor provides normal force $N_f$ and friction $f_f$. Assume the ladder is on the verge of slipping, with both floor and wall friction at their maximum values: $f_f=\mu_f N_f$ and $f_w=\mu_w N_w$, with $\mu_f=\mu_w=0.40$.

(a) Write all four equilibrium equations: ΣF_x=0, ΣF_y=0, and Στ=0 about the base (three independent equations; note this problem has more unknowns — $N_f, f_f, N_w, f_w$ — so the two friction-maximum conditions $f_f=\mu_fN_f$ and $f_w=\mu_wN_w$ are needed as additional equations to close the system).
(b) Solve the system for $N_f$, $N_w$, $f_f$, and $f_w$.
(c) Verify all four equations are satisfied by your solution.
