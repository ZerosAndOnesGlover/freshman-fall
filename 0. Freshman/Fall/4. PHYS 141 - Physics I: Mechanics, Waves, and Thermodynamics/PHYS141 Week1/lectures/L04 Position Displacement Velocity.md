# Position, Displacement & Velocity

> **Core Principle:** Motion is the change of position over time. To describe motion precisely, you need a reference point, a direction convention, and a clock. Everything else in kinematics follows from these three choices — and from calculus.

## Where This Fits

**Previously:** **L03** gave us vector algebra. Position is a vector from an origin; displacement is the change in that vector.

## 1. The Coordinate System as a Physical Choice

Before describing any motion, you must establish:

1. **An origin** — the reference point from which all positions are measured
2. **A positive direction** — typically "rightward" or "upward" by convention, but you are free to choose either
3. **A time origin** — usually t = 0 at the start of the motion of interest

These choices are **entirely yours**. The physics doesn't care. But once chosen, you must be **absolutely consistent** for the entire problem. A sign error almost always traces back to an inconsistent coordinate convention somewhere in the algebra.

> **Deep Why:** The laws of physics are the same regardless of where you place your origin or which direction you call positive. This is not a coincidence — it reflects a deep symmetry of nature called **translational invariance** (the physics doesn't depend on where you are) and **parity invariance** (roughly, the physics doesn't depend on which direction you call positive). We'll formalize this idea when we discuss conservation laws.

## 2. Position, Displacement, and Distance

### 2.1 Position: x(t)

The **position** of an object is its coordinate on your chosen axis at a given time. It is a **signed scalar in 1D** (positive or negative depending on which side of the origin the object is on).

Notation: x(t) — position as a function of time.

### 2.2 Displacement: Δx

**Displacement** is the *change* in position:

$$\Delta x = x_f - x_i$$

Displacement is a **vector quantity in 1D** (it has sign, indicating direction). It can be negative, positive, or zero.

### 2.3 Distance: d

**Distance** is the total path length traveled — always ≥ 0, always a scalar. Distance is **not** the magnitude of displacement if the object reverses direction.

### Critical Distinction

A runner completes a 400 m lap and returns to the start:
- **Displacement:** Δx = 0 m (same start and end position)
- **Distance:** d = 400 m (total path length)

If a car drives 3 km east, then 1 km west:
- **Displacement:** Δx = +2 km (eastward)
- **Distance:** d = 4 km

This distinction will matter when we define **work** (which depends on displacement) vs. heat generated by friction (which depends on distance).

---

## 3. Average Velocity

$$\bar{v} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{x_f - x_i}{t_f - t_i}$$

Average velocity is a **vector** (signed in 1D). Its sign indicates the net direction of motion.

**Average speed** is distance / time — always positive, always a scalar.

### Example

A car travels 60 km north in 1 hour, then 40 km south in 0.5 hours.
- Total displacement: +60 - 40 = +20 km (north)
- Total time: 1.5 hours
- **Average velocity:** +20/1.5 = +13.3 km/h (north)
- Total distance: 100 km
- **Average speed:** 100/1.5 = 66.7 km/h

## 4. Instantaneous Velocity — The Derivative

Average velocity over a finite time interval tells you about the *net* motion. But what is the velocity **at a single instant**?

The answer is the **limit** as the time interval shrinks to zero:

$$v(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{dx}{dt}$$

This is the **derivative of position with respect to time**. This is where calculus enters physics — not as a mathematical luxury, but as the only precise definition of "velocity at an instant."

> **Deep Why:** Before calculus, people argued for centuries about whether "instantaneous velocity" was even a meaningful concept. Zeno's paradoxes (450 BC) suggested that motion itself was paradoxical. Newton and Leibniz invented calculus specifically to resolve this: instantaneous velocity is the limit of the ratio Δx/Δt as Δt→0. The derivative is the answer to Zeno.

### Geometric Interpretation

On an x(t) graph:
- **Average velocity** over [t₁, t₂] = **slope of the secant line** connecting (t₁, x₁) and (t₂, x₂)
- **Instantaneous velocity** at time t = **slope of the tangent line** to x(t) at that point

This is the fundamental geometric meaning of the derivative.

## 5. Common Position Functions and Their Velocities

| x(t) | v(t) = dx/dt | Physical Situation |
|------|-------------|-------------------|
| x = x₀ (constant) | v = 0 | Object at rest |
| x = x₀ + v₀t | v = v₀ (constant) | Constant velocity motion |
| x = x₀ + v₀t + ½at² | v = v₀ + at | Constant acceleration (derived in Lecture 5) |
| x = A sin(ωt) | v = Aω cos(ωt) | Simple harmonic motion (Week 8) |
| x = x₀ e^(−βt) | v = −βx₀ e^(−βt) | Exponential decay (damped motion) |

## 6. Velocity as the Slope of x(t) — Reading Graphs

This is a skill you must develop until it's automatic.

**Key rules for reading x(t) graphs:**

| Feature of x(t) graph | Physical meaning |
|-----------------------|-----------------|
| Slope is positive | Object moving in +x direction |
| Slope is negative | Object moving in −x direction |
| Slope is zero (horizontal) | Object is momentarily at rest |
| Slope is steep | Object moving fast |
| Slope is shallow | Object moving slowly |
| Slope is constant | Constant velocity (no acceleration) |
| Slope is increasing | Positive acceleration |
| Slope is decreasing | Negative acceleration (could be slowing down in +x, or speeding up in −x) |
| Curve is concave up (∪ shape) | Positive acceleration (v increasing) |
| Curve is concave down (∩ shape) | Negative acceleration (v decreasing) |

---

## 7. The Inverse: Position from Velocity

If velocity is the derivative of position, then position is the **antiderivative (integral)** of velocity:

$$x(t) = x_0 + \int_{0}^{t} v(t')\, dt'$$

**Geometric meaning:** The displacement from t=0 to t=T equals the **area under the v(t) curve** between 0 and T.

This is the **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus** in action: integration and differentiation are inverses of each other, and both have direct physical meaning in kinematics.

## 8. Worked Examples

**Example 4.1:** A particle's position is given by x(t) = 3t² − 2t + 5 (in meters, t in seconds).

Find: (a) position at t = 2 s; (b) average velocity from t = 0 to t = 2 s; (c) instantaneous velocity at t = 2 s.

**(a)** x(2) = 3(4) − 2(2) + 5 = 12 − 4 + 5 = **13 m**

**(b)** x(0) = 5 m. Δx = 13 − 5 = 8 m. Δt = 2 s. **v̄ = 4 m/s**

**(c)** v(t) = dx/dt = 6t − 2. At t = 2: v(2) = 12 − 2 = **10 m/s**

---

**Example 4.2:** A car's velocity-time graph shows v = 0 at t = 0, increasing linearly to v = 20 m/s at t = 5 s, then remaining constant at 20 m/s until t = 10 s.

Find the total displacement from t = 0 to t = 10 s.

**Phase 1 (0 to 5 s):** Area under v(t) = triangle = ½ × base × height = ½ × 5 × 20 = **50 m**

**Phase 2 (5 to 10 s):** Area = rectangle = 5 × 20 = **100 m**

**Total displacement = 150 m**

## 9. Summary

| Quantity | Symbol | Definition | SI unit |
|---------|--------|-----------|---------|
| Position | x(t) | Signed coordinate | m |
| Displacement | Δx = xf − xi | Change in position | m |
| Distance | d | Path length (always ≥ 0) | m |
| Average velocity | v̄ | Δx/Δt | m/s |
| Average speed | — | d/Δt | m/s |
| Instantaneous velocity | v(t) | dx/dt | m/s |
| Instantaneous speed | |v(t)| | magnitude of v | m/s |

## CS Connection — Derivatives as Finite Differences

Instantaneous velocity is a limit; every computer that computes it uses a finite difference, `(x[i+1] − x[i]) / dt`. That approximation is the foundation of numerical differentiation and of every physics engine in every game you have played.
