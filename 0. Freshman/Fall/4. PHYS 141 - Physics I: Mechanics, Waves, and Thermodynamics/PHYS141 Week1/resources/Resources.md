## Simulations

- **PhET "Moving Man"** — Interactively control position, velocity, or acceleration and watch the other two graphs respond in real time. This is the single best tool for building intuition about x(t), v(t), a(t) relationships. Use it until reading these graphs feels effortless.
- **PhET "Projectile Motion"** — Anticipates Week 2 but useful now for free fall (set horizontal velocity to zero).

Both free at phet.colorado.edu.

## Deeper Reading

- Galileo, *Dialogues Concerning Two New Sciences* (1638) — Book III, "On Local Motion." This is the original derivation of the kinematic equations, by the physicist who discovered them — without calculus (Newton hadn't invented it yet). It's genuinely readable and historically remarkable.
- Feynman, *Feynman Lectures on Physics*, Vol. 1, Chapter 8: "Motion." Feynman's treatment of the derivative as a physical concept is unmatched.

## Common Pitfalls This Week

1. **Not establishing a coordinate system.** Every problem: state your origin and positive direction before writing a single equation.

2. **Using the kinematic equations when acceleration isn't constant.** Check the problem statement. If acceleration varies with time, you need integration — the 5 equations will give wrong answers.

3. **Confusing distance and displacement.** Displacement is signed and path-independent. Distance is path-dependent and always ≥ 0. Kinematic equations use displacement, not distance.

4. **Ignoring the quadratic's second root.** When solving Δx = v₀t + ½at², you get a quadratic with (often) two solutions. Both may be physically meaningful. Always check both.

5. **Mixing sign conventions mid-problem.** If you take up as positive, g = −9.81 m/s² throughout — do not switch to g = +9.81 m/s² partway through.
