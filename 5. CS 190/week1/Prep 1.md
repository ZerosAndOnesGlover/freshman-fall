### Part A — Turing Reading Response

#### 1. Two objections and Turing's responses

**The Mathematical Objection (Gödel).** *Turing's response:* Gödel's incompleteness results do show
that any sufficiently powerful formal system has limits, but they establish no difference in kind
between a machine and a mind — the same limits would apply to human reasoning if it were mechanical,
and no one has shown that it is not.

**Lady Lovelace's Objection.** *Turing's response:* "the machine can only do what we order it to do"
is an empirical claim for which Lovelace had no evidence, and it is false in the sense that matters —
machines, especially learning machines, can and do surprise the people who built them.

#### 2. Why the response to the Mathematical Objection is insufficient

Turing's reply only works if the human mind *is* a finite formal system, because his entire answer is
"the limitation applies to minds too." That assumption is precisely what the Mathematical Objection
denies, so the response assumes the conclusion instead of meeting the objection. A defender of the
objection can concede that some machine limits mirror human ones and still hold that Gödel's theorem
describes a positive human capacity — grasping the truth of a sentence the formal system cannot
prove — that no machine possesses. To make the rebuttal bite, Turing owes an independent argument
that the mind is mechanical, and in the 1950 paper he does not give one.

*Credit: awarded*

### Part B — Historical Turning Point

I would choose the **stored-program concept** that Turing formalized in 1936 as the Universal Turing
Machine and von Neumann described in the 1945 EDVAC report. Every other item on the timeline is,
however impressive, a faster or cheaper way of doing something that was already conceivable; the
stored program changed *what a computer is*, by making the machine's behaviour a function of data in
its memory rather than of its wiring. Without it there is no such thing as a "program" that can move
between machines, and therefore no software industry, no applications, no operating systems, and no
compilers as products — each machine would be a special-purpose calculator rebuilt for each task,
like Babbage's Difference Engine. The transistor made computing cheap and the Internet made it
connected, but both presuppose a general-purpose artifact worth manufacturing and connecting, and
that artifact is the stored-program machine. Remove it and the rest of the timeline has nothing to
scale.

*Credit: awarded*

---

### Part C — Lovelace's Claim

I land on **(b): still true in spirit but technically complicated by modern AI**, and the
complication is about what "order it to perform" means. A trained neural network still originates no
ends of its own — it has no goal except the objective it was given, and it cannot decide to pursue
something the training process did not reward, so in the respect Lovelace cared about (the machine
invents nothing) she remains right. What breaks down is the phrase "what we *know* how to order it to
perform": we no longer specify the performance, we specify a process and an objective, and the
behaviour that emerges is something nobody ordered and often nobody can predict. So the machine is
not originating ends, but it is producing means we did not and could not have written out in advance.
That is a real change, but it is a change in the relationship between the order and the performance,
not a refutation of the claim that the machine serves an end it was handed. Lovelace would, I think,
count that as within her claim's spirit even as it strains its letter.

*Credit: awarded*
