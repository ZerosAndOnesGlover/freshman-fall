# CS 202 · Week 3 · Summary

**Topic —** Synchronization: races, atomic instructions and spinlocks, futexes and sleeping locks, condition variables, semaphores, and the dining philosophers, readers–writers and sleeping barber problems.
**Lectures —** L10 Races, and the Hardware That Makes Locks Possible; L11 Sleeping Locks, Futexes, and When to Spin; L12 Condition Variables, Semaphores, and the Classic Problems.
**Work —** PS 3 (100, due Friday of Week 4), Quiz 3 (Monday, covers Week 2), Lab 3 (unmarked, sat Tuesday of Week 4). **Midterm 1 announced**: Monday of Week 4, Weeks 0–3.
**Takeaway —** `counter++` is three instructions, and two threads lost 49.9% of their increments — 37% even on one CPU, where one preemption erases a whole slice of another thread's work — while a thousand increments lost nothing, so every short test passes. Locks are built from atomic instructions; a shared atomic counter still slowed fourfold from one thread to two as its cache line bounced, where per-thread counters scaled perfectly. Uncontended, glibc's mutex made zero system calls in fifty million operations; a sleeping lock that always wakes cost 639 ns, and Drepper's three-state futex mutex cost 14.8 ns and entered the kernel in 0.35% of contended rounds — while a version that counted waiters outside its atomic word hung ten runs in ten. Spinning lost by 2.6× with four threads on one CPU and won by 20% on four. Condition variables need `while` (with `if`, consumers woke to an empty buffer hundreds of times) and one condition each (a shared one deadlocked within ten items); naive philosophers deadlocked every run and ordered forks never; glibc's default reader–writer lock gave a writer no acquisitions in four seconds. Removing xv6's allocator lock handed one physical page to two processes and then panicked the kernel.
