# CS 202 · Week 4 · Summary

**Topic —** Deadlock: the four conditions, resource-allocation graphs, diagnosis from `/proc` and `gdb`, prevention, avoidance with the Banker's algorithm, detection and recovery, livelock — and Midterm 1.
**Lectures —** L13 Deadlock: What It Takes, and What It Looks Like; L14 Preventing Deadlock, and Avoiding It with the Banker's Algorithm; L15 Detection, Recovery, Livelock — and Ignoring the Problem.
**Work —** **Midterm 1** (Monday evening, Weeks 0–3, 12.5%), PS 4 (100, due Friday of Week 5), Quiz 4 (Monday, covers Week 3), Lab 4 (unmarked, sat Tuesday of Week 5).
**Takeaway —** Two threads taking two locks in opposite orders deadlocked in every run, typically within a few hundred rounds and within about nine once each held its first lock slightly longer; all four of Coffman's conditions held, and the cycle could be read straight from `/proc` — both threads in state `S`, asleep in `futex` on the address of a mutex the other owned — and named by `gdb`, which on these machines must start the program because `ptrace_scope` is 1. Taking the locks in one order removed circular wait: thirty seconds and 199 million rounds without a stall. The Banker's algorithm reproduced the textbook, refusing a small, available request because it could not prove the result safe; its check costs O(n²m) — 21 ms for 3,200 processes — and on random workloads it prevented deadlocks that naive granting suffered 42% of the time, at the price of refusing free units. Detection with several instances found no deadlock in a state with nothing free, and four deadlocked processes after one extra request. Linux ignores user-space deadlocks — its hung-task detector watches only uninterruptible sleep — and releasing a lock to avoid deadlock produced livelock: 32 failed attempts per success, cured by random back-off.
