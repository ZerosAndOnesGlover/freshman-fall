# CS 202 · Week 2 · Summary

**Topic —** CPU scheduling: metrics and the classic policies, MLFQ, proportional share and what Linux runs now, real-time scheduling, and what an ordinary account may ask the kernel for.
**Lectures —** L07 What a Scheduler Is For, and the Classic Policies; L08 MLFQ, Fair Share, and What Linux Runs Now; L09 Real Time, and What Linux Lets You Ask For.
**Work —** PS 2 (100, due Friday of Week 3), Quiz 2 (Monday, covers Week 1), Lab 2 (unmarked, sat Tuesday of Week 3).
**Takeaway —** Scheduling goals conflict, and a simulator shows by how much: on one convoy FCFS averages 110 ms turnaround and SJF 50, round robin's quantum slides between response and switches, and an editor beside a batch job waits 10.0 ms per keystroke under FCFS, 1.3 under RR, 0.4 under MLFQ and 0.3 under a fair scheduler. MLFQ's naive rule is gameable — a job blocking just before its quantum finished in 241 ms against an honest job's 400 — until the allotment is charged regardless of blocking; and without a boost a long job waited 800 ms for 80 ms of work. On Linux, nice values divided a CPU within 0.2 percentage points of the weight table, but the kernel is EEVDF, not the CFS every textbook describes, and its slice stayed at 3.00 ms whether two or four hogs competed. Autogroup is enabled and inert because these tasks are already below a CPU cgroup, and a cgroup weight let a nice-19 hog take 58% of a CPU. Real-time classes and negative nice are refused to an ordinary account; rate-monotonic missed a deadline at 7 ms where EDF did not; and wake-up latency was 50 µs of deliberate timer slack plus 4 µs, with 90 µs more when the CPU had been idle.
