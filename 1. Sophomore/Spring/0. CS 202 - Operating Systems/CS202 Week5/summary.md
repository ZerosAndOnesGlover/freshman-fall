# CS 202 · Week 5 · Summary

**Topic —** Memory management, physical and virtual: address spaces, xv6's two-level and x86-64's four-level page tables, the TLB, huge pages, PCIDs and shootdowns, demand paging, copy-on-write, and the physical allocators underneath.
**Lectures —** L16 Address Spaces and Page Tables; L17 The TLB: Making Translation Fast; L18 Demand Paging, Copy-on-Write, and Physical Memory.
**Work —** PS 4 due Friday; PS 5 (100, released Wednesday, due Friday of Week 6); Quiz 5 (Monday, covers Week 4); Lab 4 (sat Tuesday of this week); Lab 5 (unmarked, sat Tuesday of Week 6).
**Takeaway —** Every address a program uses is translated by the CPU through tables the kernel owns. xv6 splits 32-bit addresses 10/10/12 and walks two levels in `walkpgdir`; a fork of a 4 MiB xv6 process took exactly 1,096 pages, 65 of them to map the kernel into the child. x86-64 splits 48-bit addresses into four 9-bit indices; user space ends one page below 2⁴⁷, and this kernel supports five levels on a CPU that has four. Linux's tables follow use: a gigabyte mapped cost nothing, touched cost 2 MiB, and 64 scattered pages cost 512 KiB. This CPU's TLBs hold 64 + 1,536 small-page translations, 6 MiB of reach; beyond that, page walks cost 1.6 ns per access at 4 MiB and 17 ns at 2 GiB, which 2 MiB pages removed. With PCIDs a process switch cost only 1–4% more than a thread switch, and unmapping memory while another thread ran on another CPU sent a shootdown interrupt every time, 11–20% of the operation. Linux allocates on first touch — about 1.9 µs per page, then 14 ns — maps unread memory to a shared zero page, and forks a gigabyte in 20 ms by copying tables and write-protecting them; xv6 allocates every page eagerly, copies everything on fork, lets a program overwrite its own code, and kills any process that faults. The buddy allocator splits large blocks to supply small pages and does not always merge them back, and frame numbers have been hidden from users since Rowhammer.
