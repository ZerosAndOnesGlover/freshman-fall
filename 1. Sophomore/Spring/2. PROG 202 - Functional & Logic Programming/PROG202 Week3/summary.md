# PROG 202 · Week 3 · Summary

**Topic —** Lazy evaluation: thunks, weak head normal form, strictness, space leaks, and infinite lists. The week that explains every measurement in Weeks 0–2.
**Lectures —** L07 Thunks, Weak Head Normal Form, and Where the Memory Went; L08 Infinite Lists, and Laziness as the Other Kind of Glue.
**Work —** PS 3 (100, fifteen parts, due Friday of Week 4), Quiz 3 (Tuesday, ten minutes, covers Week 2, unmarked). **Lab 2 is sat this Wednesday**; Lab 3 is sat Wednesday of Week 4. **Required reading: Hughes, *Why Functional Programming Matters* (1989), all 23 pages — on the final.**
**Takeaway —** A thunk is a heap object holding unrun code; entering it runs the code and **overwrites the thunk**, which is why the second request is free and why `fibs !! 1000` is instant. Forcing goes to **weak head normal form — the outermost constructor — and stops**: measured, `p = (1+1, 2+2)` then `p \`seq\` ()` leaves `:sprint p` showing **`(_,_)` unchanged**, and `seq` on a list yields `_ : _`, one cell with both parts unevaluated. `deepseq` reaches normal form; `seq`, `$!` and `!` do not. That distinction is worth **727 MB**: the one-pass mean written with `foldl'` — the *strict* fold — holds 727 MB at `-O0`, because the accumulator is a pair and WHNF of a pair is the constructor, so two ten-million-link thunk chains grow inside it. Bang patterns fix it (60 KB) and `data Acc = Acc !Int !Int` fixes it *and* removes the allocation (110 KB against 160 MB at `-O2`). **Two different problems must not be merged:** the two-pass version leaks **at both optimisation levels** (245 MB, 289 MB) because it is *retention* — a name keeps evaluated data reachable, and no `seq` touches that — while the 53 KB-against-640 MB allocation gap is *fusion*, which naming the list switches off. **Strictness analysis** is why `-O2` turned `foldl` from 619 MB into 44 KB, and it is an optimisation rather than a guarantee, which is why `foldl'` — which says `seq` out loud — is the rule. `foldr` with a strict operator cannot be repaired at all: `1 + (2 + (3 + …))` must reach the list's end before any addition, and what is held is the **evaluation stack**, not a heap chain. The result the optimiser does **not** save you from: `Data.Map.Lazy` as a counter holds **107 MB** at `-O2` where `Data.Map.Strict` holds **44 KB**, because the analyser cannot see through a balanced tree. Lab 3's payoff is that moving five `!`s from a function into the **type** fixed the function nobody edited. On the other side, laziness buys what Hughes claims: `[1..]` is an ordinary value, `fibs = 0 : 1 : zipWith (+) fibs (tail fibs)` is legal because it is **productive** (remove the seeds and it is `<<loop>>`), and `sqrts` produces approximations for ever while `within 1e-12` decides when to stop — **six computed out of infinitely many, with neither function knowing about the other.** Generate-and-test searches an infinite space and terminates (`head` over `[1..]` gives `(3,4,5)`), which is Week 9's execution model arriving early. And the famous two-line `primes` is **not the Sieve of Eratosthenes** — it divides where a sieve adds — and is **19× slower** than a real one at the ten-thousandth prime: elegant, correct, famous, wrong algorithm, with nothing in its shape to say so.
**New syntax —** `seq` · `$!` · `!x` bang patterns · `!Int` strict fields · `~p` lazy patterns · `deepseq`/`force` · `{-# LANGUAGE BangPatterns #-}` · `_` as a thunk in `:sprint` · `iterate`/`repeat`/`cycle` · `Data.Map.Strict` against `Data.Map.Lazy` · `insertWith` · `+RTS -K16m` · `-prof -fprof-auto`, `+RTS -hc`.
