# PROG 202 · Week 2 · Summary

**Topic —** Higher-order functions: `map`, `filter`, composition, currying, and the two folds — with the measurements that say which fold is wrong and when.
**Lectures —** L05 Higher-Order Functions: What Is Left of a Loop When You Take the Variable Away; L06 `foldr`, `foldl`, `foldl'` — and Measuring Which One Is Wrong.
**Work —** PS 2 (100, fifteen parts, due Friday of Week 3), Quiz 2 (Tuesday, ten minutes, covers Week 1, unmarked). **Lab 1 is sat this Wednesday**; Lab 2 is sat Wednesday of Week 3.
**Takeaway —** A function is a value and that is all "higher-order" means. `f x y` is `(f x) y` because `->` is right-associative and application is left-associative, so partial application is free and `map (add 3)` needs no machinery. `foldr` replaces a list's own constructors — every `:` becomes `f`, `[]` becomes `z` — and its **universal property** (any function with those two equations *is* a `foldr`) is what lets `f . map g` be rewritten as one fold, which is the identity behind GHC's fusion. Then the measurements, which invert the received advice. Summing `[1..10⁷]` at **`-O2`: `foldr` 130 MB, `foldl` 44 KB, `foldl'` 44 KB**; at **`-O0`: `foldr` 446 MB, `foldl` 619 MB, `foldl'` 44 KB** — **the ranking of the two plain folds reverses**, because `-O2`'s strictness analyser turns `foldl` into `foldl'` and cannot help `foldr` at all. **Nothing overflows the stack** at either level: GHC's stack lives on the heap and grows to 80% of it, so the textbook's crash needs `+RTS -K16m` to reproduce — and then **both** folds overflow, which shows the advice was always about *strictness*, not direction. `foldl'` alone is 44 KB in all four cells, so it is the only defensible default for a strict operator; **`foldl` without the prime is never the answer.** For a lazy operator the answer flips: `foldr (\x acc -> x > 5 || acc) False [1..]` returns **`True`** immediately, and both left folds never terminate (`and (repeat False)` gives **`<<loop>>`**, the Week 0 blackhole), because `foldr`'s outermost call is the *first* element and `foldl`'s involves the *last*. Two costs measured alongside: `acc ++ [x]` inside a fold is quadratic — **22.57 s against 0.01 s at n = 40,000**, 2,257× — because `(++)` re-walks its whole left argument, and that is Hutton §7.3's own `reverse`. And Hutton's four-line `qsort` on a million random `Int`s takes **1.75 s against `Data.List.sort`'s 3.24 s** — it is *faster* than the library — while on already-sorted input it takes **19.0 s at n = 40,000** and worse than quadratic, where the library does a million in **0.194 s** because a bottom-up mergesort sees one run. The average case is better and the worst case is the input you actually get.
