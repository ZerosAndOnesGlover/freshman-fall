**Takeaway —** A presentation serves three audiences who want different things, so the order is **show it works, show it is trustworthy, say what you got wrong** — and note that **the demo is 10 marks of 100**, worth doing well because a failure damages the other 90 by association rather than because the stage is where the marks are. **Show the refusal**: book a slot, be refused, and say in one sentence where the guarantee lives — thirteen weeks in ninety seconds. **Show that your tests are worth something** — a mutation score with its line count, the survivors you read and what they were, **then break something live and let a test catch it**, which answers the published viva question by doing it. **The strongest part of an engineering presentation is where you were wrong**, because everyone in the room has been wrong the same way and the pretence is more visible than the mistake; *"communication could have been better"* is what people say instead of a finding. **Brooks's arithmetic**: four people is 6 pairs, five is 10, so a fifth member adds 25% more labour and 67% more pairs — and **the most robust result in the course is batch size**, arriving from three separate literatures in review size, TDD's small steps and deployment frequency. **Project Aristotle found individual ability did not predict team performance and psychological safety did**, by a wide margin — one organisation, self-reported, causation unestablished — **and it is what makes code review possible**, which is why thirteen weeks of assignments awarded marks for *"what you are unsure about"* and why four-character severity labels make the safety cheap. **Estimation fails structurally**: the variance is in the steps you cannot foresee, overruns are long-tailed, and an estimate becomes a commitment when repeated — so make things small, count rather than estimate, give cycle-time ranges, and **the professional skill is communicating uncertainty without being useless.** A **blameless post-mortem protects the information, not the feelings**, in five sections of which *"what made it hard to detect or fix"* produces the valuable actions; **human error is a symptom, not a cause**, and *"deployment was manual and unverified"* is the cause because only it has an action. **Everything in the top half of the adoptability table is unilateral** — small changes, tests for what you change, writing down decisions, characterisation tests — so **do not wait for a mandate to write a test**; for the rest, bring evidence rather than principles. **The two senior tracks differ in what your output is and in feedback-loop length**, hours-to-weeks against months-to-years, which after a term arguing that short loops are everything is the honest difficulty. **The four things this course was for:** code you cannot change safely is worthless however correct; every practice is a claim about cost, in a context, with evidence — **and four originators disowned what their ideas became, because the memorable name travels and the caveats do not**; instrument what you intend to act on and act on it; and the hard part is other people and it is mostly mechanical. **The one sentence: you are not optimising for a program that is correct — you are optimising for a program that can be changed by someone who did not write it, years after you have left.**
