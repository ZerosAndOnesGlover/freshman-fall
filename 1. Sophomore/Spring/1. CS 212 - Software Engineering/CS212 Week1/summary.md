# CS 212 · Week 1 · Summary

**Topic —** Requirements engineering: the four categories and the invariant that is in none of them; user stories and acceptance criteria that include the failure cases; use cases and their extensions; the domain model as vocabulary, and where an invariant is enforced.
**Lectures —** L04 What a Requirement Is, and Why Yours Are Wrong; L05 User Stories, and What Makes One Worth Writing; L06 Use Cases, and the Domain Model.
**Work —** A 0 due Friday 17:00; A 1 released Wednesday (100, due Friday of Week 2); **Quiz 1 Tuesday** (unmarked, covers Week 0); **the walking skeleton, Friday** (unmarked this week, 70% of Phase 1 depends on it).
**Takeaway —** The VNC 101 double-booking was a requirement nobody wrote down, and the category it belongs to — the **invariant**, a sentence that must never become false — appears in no user story because it never occurs to anyone that it could be false. Requirements are discovered rather than gathered: people describe their workaround rather than their goal, tacit rules are invisible to their holders, preferences are constructed while you ask, and conflict hides as vagueness — *"admins should be able to cancel bookings"* is seven words and five unsettled questions, because the registrar wants control and the lecturer wants not to be overridden. The highest-yield interview question is *"tell me about the last time this went wrong"*. A story is Card, Conversation and Confirmation, and the card exists only to schedule the conversation; INVEST's load-bearing letters are **Small** and **Valuable**; stories split by workflow step, business rule, happy path, data variation or interface effort, and **never by layer**, because a layer split rebuilds the waterfall inside the sprint. The acceptance criteria are the requirement, and the concurrent-request scenario is the entire difference between `slot` and `roomsvc`. A use case is a story with its failure paths: booking a resource has one success path and **six extensions**, and extension 6a — a notification failure must not roll back a confirmed booking — is a bug `roomsvc` actually has at `bookings.py:398`. A domain model is a vocabulary before it is a structure, and `roomsvc` has **five words for one concept** with three incompatible representations. **The week's argument:** fixed-duration slots make the invariant an equality a unique index enforces; arbitrary intervals make it an overlap no unique index expresses — so the modelling decision and the enforceability decision are one decision, and the more general model is the weaker one.
