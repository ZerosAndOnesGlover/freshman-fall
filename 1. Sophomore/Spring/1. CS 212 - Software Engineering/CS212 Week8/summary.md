# CS 212 · Week 8 · Summary

**Topic —** Continuous integration and deployment: the practice against the server; containers and the image you ship; deploying so that failure is cheap.
**Lectures —** L25 Continuous Integration — the Practice, and the Server; L26 Docker, and the Image You Ship; L27 Deployment, and How to Fail Safely.
**Work —** **A 7 due Friday 27 March**; A 8 released Wednesday (100, almost every mark for something that runs, due Friday 3 April); **Quiz 8 Tuesday** (unmarked, covers Week 7). First week back from Spring Break.
**Takeaway —** **CI is a practice, not a server**: the load-bearing words in Fowler's definition are *integrate* and *daily*, and a team with a green badge and four fortnight-old branches has automated testing with deferred integration — test it with `git log --oneline main --since='7 days ago'` and the age of your oldest branch. Long-lived branches cost **semantic drift**, not merge conflicts, because a clean merge is not a correct merge; merge unfinished work as unrouted code, behind a feature flag, or by branch-by-abstraction — **and Knight Capital's flag was repurposed**, so delete flags when they ship and never reuse a name. **Build speed decides everything**: over 30 minutes a team batches pushes to avoid the wait, which is deferred integration caused by the pipeline meant to prevent it — `roomsvc` is 94 s of tests inside a 6:40 pipeline, of which 4:10 is uncached dependency install. **The two-speed pipeline** puts fast deterministic checks in a blocking gate and everything slow or probabilistic in a non-blocking nightly sweep, **and a sweep nobody reads is worse than none.** **DORA, ~2,000 organisations: deployment frequency and lead time move *with* low failure rates and fast recovery**, and the mechanism is **batch size** — the same mechanism as review size and TDD's small steps, which is three literatures agreeing. A container is **a process on your kernel** with namespaces, cgroups and a union filesystem — not a VM, and no kernel to boot is why it starts in milliseconds; it buys a reproducible filesystem and an artefact, and **does not buy "works on my machine" immunity** (environment variables, volume mounts hiding baked-in source, build context, architecture, moving base tags), VM-grade isolation, or **any architectural property**. The Dockerfile's five decisions are dependency layers before source, multi-stage, **non-root**, **no config baked in**, and a healthcheck; Compose's three load-bearing lines are `tmpfs`, `condition: service_healthy`, and the service name as hostname. **Build once, tag with the commit SHA, promote that image** — a rebuild resolves dependencies again, so the thing you tested and the thing you deployed differ and nothing can tell you. **Migrations are the hard part**: a separate step, backward-compatible with running code — **five deploys to rename a column** — and reversed at least once, or the rollback plan is fiction. **Knight Capital is five failures with five cheap answers**, and **deployment verification is nine lines of YAML**: assert every target reports the SHA you built. Strategies matter less than **reversal time** — *if this is wrong, how do we get back, and how long?* — and a migration that drops a column restores in never. Observability is added **before** the incident: a correlation ID, structured logs, and four numbers including **one domain metric**, because a deploy can return 200 everywhere while silently rejecting every booking. **An alert must say what to do** — the fourth appearance in eight weeks of one disease: a signal tolerated until it stopped carrying information.
