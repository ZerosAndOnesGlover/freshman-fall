**Takeaway —** A shell is a loop, a parser and a job table around `fork`, `exec`, `wait`, `dup2` and `pipe` — all of which you already had. Tokenise on whitespace **and** operators, longest first, and keep a copy of the line because the tokeniser destroys it; redirections belong to the pipeline, not the stage; reject bad syntax before forking anything. `cd` is a builtin because it **cannot** be anything else, and `echo` is one because an external command costs **955 µs against under 5 µs** — of which `fork` is only **127 µs**, `exec` is the rest, and **147 µs is the dynamic linker** (a statically linked trivial binary ran in 532 µs against 679). Then the new idea: **session ⊃ process group ⊃ process**, with at most one foreground group. `fork` inherits the group; `setpgid(0,0)` makes a new one and puts you in the background; **`setsid()` loses the controlling terminal entirely** — `tcgetpgrp` returns −1 — which is how a daemon detaches. A pipeline is **one group** led by its first stage, and a shell calls `setpgid` in **both** parent and child because either alone is a race. The terminal gives a background group three different answers: **`read` always stops it (`SIGTTIN`), `write` does not unless `TOSTOP` is set, and `tcsetattr` always does (`SIGTTOU`)** — which is why a shell must ignore `SIGTTOU` around its own `tcsetpgrp` or stop itself. **Ctrl-C is `kill(-foreground_pgid, SIGINT)` sent by the terminal driver**, and the shell implements it by not being in that group. Job control needs **`WUNTRACED`** — without it a shell hangs the first time a job stops — and reaping must loop, because `SIGCHLD` does not queue. And `getpgid` on a pid `waitpid` has just reaped returns **`ESRCH`**: after a wait, the only thing you still know is the status.
