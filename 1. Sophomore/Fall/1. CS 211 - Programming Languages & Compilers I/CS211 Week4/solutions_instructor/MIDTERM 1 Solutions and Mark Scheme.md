## Q1 · Grammars and Ambiguity (15)

### (a) [3] The four components

$G = (N, \Sigma, P, S)$ — **non-terminals, terminals, productions, start symbol.**

**1 mark per three correct.** All four for 3. **Do not accept** "rules and symbols".

### (b) [4] All trees for `9 - 5 - 2`

**Two trees.**

| Tree | Value |
|---|---:|
| `((9 - 5) - 2)` | **2** |
| `(9 - (5 - 2))` | **6** |

**2 for both trees drawn, 2 for both values.** **−2** for only one tree. **A student drawing three or more has confused derivation order with tree shape** — worth a written comment, as it recurs in Q3.

### (c) [4] Left-associative

```bnf
E ::= E "-" T | T
T ::= n
```

**The feature: left recursion.** The recursive reference is on the *left* of the operator, so the left operand may itself be a subtraction while the right may not — forcing `((9-5)-2)`.

**2 grammar, 2 for naming left recursion as the cause.** **1 of 2** for "because it groups left" without connecting to the recursion side.

### (d) [4] Is the *language* ambiguous?

**No.**

**Ambiguity is a property of a grammar, not of a language.** The same language is generated by a stratified grammar that gives exactly one tree per string. A language is ambiguous only if *every* grammar generating it is — a much stronger and rarer condition.

**2 for "no", 2 for the grammar/language distinction.** **A student who answers "yes" earns 0 even with good reasoning** — the distinction is the entire point of the question, and it was made explicitly in L02 §5 and Week 2's README.

**Award 3 of 4** for a student who says no and cites the stratified grammar without using the words "property of the grammar".

## Q2 · Lexical Analysis (15)

### (a) [3] Maximal munch

**At each position take the longest match. If two rules match the same longest string, take the one listed first.**

**2 for the longest-match half, 1 for the tie-break.** Both halves are required for 3.

### (b) [4] `x-->y`

**`IDENT(x)` `OP(-)` `OP(->)` `IDENT(y)`.**

**Why the first `-` stands alone:** at that position, `--` is not an operator and `->` requires the next character to be `>`, but it is `-`. **So the longest legal match is the single `-`.**

**2 for the four tokens in order, 2 for the explanation.**

### (c) [4] The blowup

**Maximum: $2^n$ states.**

**Minimisation does not avoid it.** $(a\mid b)^*a(a\mid b)^n$ attains the bound: an NFA of 58 states gives a minimal DFA of exactly 512, and minimisation removes only **one** state.

**The reason:** the machine must distinguish $2^{n+1}$ distinct histories — to know what the character $n+1$ from the end was — and no two can share a state because they lead to different futures. **The states are not redundant.**

**2 for $2^n$, 2 for a justification that appeals to distinguishable histories.** **1 of 2** for "no, because the blowup is inherent" with no argument.

### (d) [4] Bounded nesting

**False — it *is* regular.** A DFA needs about **52 states**: one per depth 0–50, plus a dead state.

**Where the impossibility proof fails:** it feeds $k+1$ strings of *unboundedly increasing* depth to force two into the same state by pigeonhole. **With depth capped at 50 there are only 51 depths to feed**, so for $k \ge 52$ the pigeonhole never fires.

**2 for "false", 1 for the state count, 1 for locating where the proof breaks.**

**This is the most-missed question on the paper.** Expect a majority to answer "true". **Accept 50, 51, 52 or 53** as the count — the exact convention for the dead state and the empty string varies.

## Q3 · Parsing (15)

### (a) [3] Left recursion

**The function calls itself as its first action, having consumed no input**, so it recurses forever without examining a token.

**3 for an answer mentioning that nothing is consumed before the recursive call.** **1** for "it loops forever" alone.

### (b) [4] Why FOLLOW

**Because of ε-productions.** At `E'` with lookahead `)`, no production of `E'` *starts* with `)` — but `E' → ε` is legal precisely when the next token can follow `E'`. **FOLLOW is the set that licenses the empty production.**

**2 for identifying ε as the reason, 2 for a concrete situation.**

### (c) [4] Shift or reduce

**Shift.**

`E + T` is the right-hand side of `E → E + T`, but reducing now would be wrong: the `*` means the `T` is unfinished and `T * F` must reduce to `T` first.

**The term: a right-hand side on the stack that must not be reduced is not a *handle*.** A handle is one whose reduction leads to a valid rightmost derivation.

**2 for "shift" with justification, 2 for the word *handle* used correctly.**

### (d) [4] What a conflict means

Either:

1. **The grammar is genuinely ambiguous** (the dangling else), or
2. **The grammar is unambiguous but not LALR(1)** — the tool could not prove determinism with one token of lookahead.

**bison decides (2).** *Is this grammar LALR(1)?* is **decidable**; *is this grammar ambiguous?* is **undecidable**, so no tool answers it in general.

**2 for both possibilities, 2 for correctly identifying which question bison decides.** **Full marks require the word *undecidable* or an equivalent.**

## Q4 · Semantic Analysis (15)

### (a) [4] Three fragments

| Fragment | Phase | Why not earlier |
|---|---|---|
| `let x = 1 @ 2;` | **Lexer** | `@` is not in Cyan's alphabet at all — no rule matches it, so tokenisation fails before any structure exists |
| `let x = (1 + 2;` | **Parser** | Every token is legal; the *arrangement* is not. The lexer has no notion of matching brackets |
| `let x = 1 + true;` | **Type checker** | Legal tokens, legal arrangement. **Not context-free** — `+`'s operand rule cannot be expressed in a grammar |

**1 per row, 1 for the overall reasoning being consistent.**

**The third row is the discriminating one.** A student who says "the parser" for `1 + true` has not absorbed L02 §8.

### (b) [4] Declaration order

**Mutual recursion works** because the checker makes **three passes** over the top level — structs, then all function signatures, then bodies. `even` is in the symbol table before `odd`'s body is examined.

**`let a = b;` fails** because statements inside a body get **one pass, in order**. When `let a = b` is checked, `b` has not been declared.

**2 per case.** **An answer of "functions are global" earns 1 of 2** — the question asks about passes.

### (c) [3] The forcing step

**The second application.** `f`'s argument type is fixed to `x`'s type by the inner `f x`; the outer application then demands `f` accept the *result* of the inner one. **Unifying those two demands forces argument and result to be the same type.**

**3 for identifying the outer application as the forcing step.** **1** for "because f is applied twice" with no mechanism.

### (d) [4] The occurs check

**Equation: $t_0 = t_0 \to t_1$.** Applying `x` to itself requires `x : t0` (as argument) and `x : t0 -> t1` (as function).

**No finite type satisfies it** — substituting repeatedly gives an infinitely growing type.

**Without the check**, the algorithm binds $t_0$ to a structure containing itself and **loops forever** the next time anything traverses the type.

**2 for the equation, 1 for the infinite-type argument, 1 for non-termination.**

## Q5 · Synthesis (15)

**This question is open. There is no required position.** Mark the reasoning.

### (a) [5] The grammar change

```ebnf
cmp_expr ::= add_expr { ( "==" | "!=" | "<" | "<=" | ">" | ">=" ) add_expr }
```

**`[ ... ]` becomes `{ ... }`** — optional becomes zero-or-more.

**4 for the production, 1 for identifying that the change is exactly optional → repeated.** Accept a left-recursive BNF equivalent.

### (b) [4] What gets harder elsewhere

**The expected answer is IR generation (Week 4), and the difficulty is evaluating the middle operand exactly once.**

`a < b < c` must mean `a < b && b < c` **with `b` evaluated a single time** — so the lowering must bind it to a temporary, and cannot simply duplicate the subtree. If `b` is `f()`, duplicating it calls `f` twice.

**Also accept** with full marks:

- **Type checking** — the rule is no longer binary; it must check a *chain* of operands pairwise.
- **The AST shape** — a `Binary` node no longer suffices; you need an n-ary comparison node, which every later phase must then handle.

**2 for naming a phase other than parsing, 2 for a specific difficulty.** **A student who says "it's more complex" earns 1.**

### (c) [6] The argument

| Position | A good answer names… |
|---|---|
| **C** | Uniformity — one rule for all binary operators, no special case. **Cost:** `if (0 <= i < n)` compiles, always passes, and the bounds bug ships |
| **Python** | It gives the answer the notation promises. **Cost:** a construct that behaves unlike every other binary operator, and a special case every tool must implement |
| **Cyan** | No wrong answer is expressible; caught at parse time with no analysis. **Cost:** a formula transcribed from a paper must be rewritten by hand, and the rewrite is where a typo enters |

**2 for a clearly stated position. 4 for the cost, and the cost must be a *concrete situation*.**

**A student who argues their position has no downside earns at most 2 of 6**, however well written. **This is stated in the paper** ("this is where the marks are") and is the syllabus's habit 2 being examined.
