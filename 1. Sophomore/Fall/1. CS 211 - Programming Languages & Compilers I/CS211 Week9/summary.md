# CS 211 · Week 9 · Summary

**Topic —** Concurrency: sequential consistency and why nothing implements it, the store buffer and x86-TSO, compiler reordering, data races as undefined behaviour, happens-before, C11 memory orders, the Java Memory Model, locks, actors, STM, async/await, and ThreadSanitizer.
**Lectures —** L19 What Concurrent Code Even Means; L20 The Models That Take the Problem Away.
**Work —** PS 9 (100, due Week 10 Friday), Lab 9 (unmarked, Friday), Quiz 9 (unmarked, Tuesday, covers Week 8). **Project 1 due Week 11.**
**Takeaway —** The outcome sequential consistency proves impossible happens 44 times in 200,000 iterations on this machine, and a `seq_cst` fence removes it completely (0 in 1.5M) — but the compiler is the more aggressive reorderer: at `-O2` gcc emitted `.L6: jmp .L6` for a racy spin-wait, a literal infinite loop, on the most strongly ordered mainstream ISA there is. Measured alongside: a data race is *undefined behaviour*, not unpredictability, which is why `-O2` turned a million-iteration racy loop into one `addq` and got the right answer in 0.000 s while `-O0` lost 44–63% of the increments — the optimiser "fixed" the bug by deleting it. Java has the identical bug in a memory-safe GC'd language and only after the JIT warms up. Costs: `seq_cst` ~12% over `relaxed`, a mutex ~3.3× a `seq_cst` atomic, actors ~13× a lock — and the racy version is always fastest and always wrong, except in CPython where the GIL hides it by accident. One prediction in the lecture was wrong and is corrected in place: removing the cache-line padding *raises* the forbidden rate ~100×, because false sharing lengthens the load and widens the window.
