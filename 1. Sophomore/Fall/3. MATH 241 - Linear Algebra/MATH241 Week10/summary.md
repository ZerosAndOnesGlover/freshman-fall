**Takeaway —** Week 7 admitted that $A = S\Lambda S^{-1}$ fails twice — $S$ may not exist, and it may exist with $\operatorname{cond}(S) = 1.3\times10^8$ — and Week 8 named the fix without saying when it was available. **It is available exactly when $A$ is symmetric, and then always:** $A = Q\Lambda Q^\mathsf{T}$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $\Lambda$ real, no hypotheses, no exceptions. Three claims are packed inside: **the eigenvalues are real** (two lines, using $A^\mathsf{T} = A$ once, and in $2\times2$ visible as a discriminant $4 + 4e^2$ that cannot go negative); **eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are perpendicular** ($(\lambda-\mu)(x\cdot y) = 0$); and **a repeated eigenvalue is never a defect**, with Gram–Schmidt supplying an orthonormal set inside the eigenspace. The payoff is that **$S^{-1}$ becomes $S^\mathsf{T}$ and $\operatorname{cond}(Q) = 1$ exactly** — Week 7's complaint answered — and that **$A = \sum\lambda_kq_kq_k^\mathsf{T}$ makes a symmetric matrix a weighted sum of perpendicular projections**, with Week 8's $P$ as one term. **The converse holds**, so the theorem characterises symmetry. Symmetry gives *real*, not *positive*; **positive definiteness is the extra condition**, and it has **five equivalent tests** — the form, the eigenvalues, the pivots, the **leading** minors, and $A = R^\mathsf{T}R$ — which flip together at exactly $b = \tfrac{32}{15}$ on a one-parameter family, located by an exact determinant that no floating-point eigenvalue could resolve. **Pivots and eigenvalues agree in sign and in nothing else** (Sylvester), and **positive diagonal entries prove nothing**: $\begin{bmatrix}1&3\\3&1\end{bmatrix}$ has both positive and is indefinite. **$A = LDL^\mathsf{T}$ shows that elimination *is* completing the square**, giving $x^\mathsf{T}Ax = \sum d_ky_k^2$ and, in two sentences, the proof that positive pivots suffice. Geometrically $x^\mathsf{T}Ax = 1$ is an ellipse with axes along the eigenvectors, semi-axes $1/\sqrt\lambda$, and **aspect ratio $\sqrt{\operatorname{cond}}$** — a round ellipse is a well-conditioned matrix, literally. **Cholesky halves the work and needs no pivoting ever**, the only factorisation in the course that does not, because the stability is a theorem about the matrix rather than a property of the algorithm; its failure is also the cheapest positive-definiteness test there is. **Week 0's Hilbert matrix is finally diagnosed** — $\lambda_\max$ barely moves while $\lambda_\min$ collapses to $10^{-17}$, and every $H_n$ is positive definite, so that adjective is not a safety guarantee. And the week's headline is a measurement: **a symmetric eigenvalue problem is perfectly conditioned** — over five perturbation sizes the eigenvalues never move further than the perturbation — while a $10\times10$ shift matrix perturbed by $10^{-10}$ in one entry sends **every** eigenvalue from $0$ to magnitude $0.1$, a **billion-fold** amplification verified exactly through $C^{10} = \varepsilon I$. **That matrix at $\varepsilon = 0$ is Week 7's single Jordan block**, so defectiveness and eigenvalue sensitivity are one phenomenon, and one hypothesis excludes both.
