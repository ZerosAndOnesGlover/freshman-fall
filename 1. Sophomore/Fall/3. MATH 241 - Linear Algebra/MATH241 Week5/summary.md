**Takeaway —** The determinant is defined by three properties — $\det I = 1$, a row swap flips the sign, and linearity **in each row separately** — and everything else is derived, including the one that matters: adding a multiple of one row to another changes nothing, which is precisely what lets elimination compute it. So $\det A$ is $\pm$ **the product of the pivots**, at $n^3/3$, and Week 1's matrix had pivots $2, 3, 4$ — its determinant was $24$ four weeks before anyone said so. $\det A = 0 \iff$ singular completes Week 1's list of seven characterisations. The $n!$ formula and cofactor expansion are exact and hopeless — $9\times10^{14}$ times slower than elimination at $n = 20$, **measured at 751× for $n$ as small as 8** — and so are the adjugate formula and Cramer's rule, which needs $n+1$ determinants to do one solve's work. **Cofactors nonetheless earn their lecture**, because they alone handle *symbolic* entries: elimination would divide by expressions that might vanish, and $\det(A - \lambda I)$ is next week's whole subject. Geometrically the three properties are the properties of **area**, so $\lvert\det A\rvert$ is the volume-scaling factor and the sign is orientation — a shear has determinant 1 by base-times-height, a reflection $-1$, a projection $0$. The product rule $\det(AB) = \det A\det B$ then gives $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A$, $\det(AB) = \det(BA)$ even though $AB \ne BA$, and **similarity invariance in one line — the theorem Week 4 assumed and deferred.** MATH 142's $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$ turns out to be this week's theorem, $r$ being a Jacobian determinant. And the standing warning is renewed with the definition now available: **$\det A = 0$ is an exact criterion and a bad numerical test**, because $\det(cA) = c^n\det A$ is not scale-invariant and $\operatorname{cond}(A)$ is.
