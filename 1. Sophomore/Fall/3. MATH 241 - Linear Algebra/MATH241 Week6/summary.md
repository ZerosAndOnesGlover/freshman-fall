**Takeaway —** An eigenvector is a nonzero $v$ with $Av = \lambda v$ — a direction the transformation does not turn — and it is the question Week 4 asked without the vocabulary when it chose the basis making a reflection diagonal. $\lambda = 0$ is allowed and means $A$ is singular; $v = 0$ is not. The point of the definition is that **$A^kv = \lambda^kv$ turns matrix powers into scalar powers**, which is why eigenvalues answer questions about iteration, stability and PageRank. Finding them is $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ — **and this is why Week 5 happened**, because that determinant has a symbol in it and elimination would divide by expressions that may vanish, so cofactor expansion is the only tool despite being $9\times10^{14}$ times too slow for numbers. The eigenspace is then $\mathbf{N}(A - \lambda I)$, by Week 2's algorithm unchanged. Every $n\times n$ matrix has exactly $n$ eigenvalues over $\mathbb{C}$, and **$\sum\lambda_i = \operatorname{trace}A$ while $\prod\lambda_i = \det A$** — so Week 4's two unexplained similarity invariants turn out to be the sum and product of a better one, since similar matrices share a characteristic polynomial. Both identities are **free checks** and should be run every time. Two things go wrong: a rotation has **no real eigenvalues**, correctly, because it fixes no direction — over $\mathbb{C}$ it has two and the trace/determinant identities survive — and a shear has a repeated eigenvalue with **only one eigenvector**. That second failure is the week's real content: **algebraic multiplicity counts root repetitions, geometric multiplicity is the eigenspace dimension, always $1 \le$ geometric $\le$ algebraic, and a gap means *defective* — no basis of eigenvectors, no diagonalisation.** A repeated eigenvalue alone does not do it: $-I$ repeats and is perfectly diagonal. And the geometric multiplicity is the invariant that finally separates $I$ from $\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, which Week 4 could only distinguish by a trick peculiar to scalar matrices.
