**Takeaway —** When $b \notin \mathbf{C}(A)$ there is no solution, and that is the normal case whenever there is more data than parameters; the useful question is not *solve $Ax = b$* but *minimise $\lVert b - Ax\rVert$*, whose answer Week 8 already supplied — **the nearest reachable point is the projection**, so solve $A\hat x = p$. Requiring the residual to be orthogonal to every column gives the **normal equations** $A^\mathsf{T}A\hat x = A^\mathsf{T}b$, replacing $m$ equations with $n$ and collapsing a million measurements to a small square solve. Fitting a line puts the parameters in $\hat x$ and the data in $A$ and $b$; the fit always passes through the centroid, and the mean of a dataset is literally the least-squares projection onto the constants. **"Squares" is a choice** — differentiable, linear, and maximum-likelihood under normal errors — and its price is that a residual of 10 counts a hundred times one of 1. The week's real content is that the normal equations are **exact mathematics and the wrong algorithm**: $\operatorname{cond}(A^\mathsf{T}A) = \operatorname{cond}(A)^2$ doubles the digits lost, and on Läuchli's matrix — two visibly independent columns — $A^\mathsf{T}A$ becomes **exactly singular in floating point** at $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$, the information destroyed by the single addition $1 + \varepsilon^2$. Substituting $A = QR$ gives $R\hat x = Q^\mathsf{T}b$ and never forms $A^\mathsf{T}A$ — **but "use QR" is not adequate advice**: measured on the same problem, **Gram–Schmidt QR fails earlier than the normal equations**, exactly as Week 8's L26 §5 predicted, and only **Householder QR** — reflections that are exactly orthogonal by construction — survives. The normal equations' apparently exact answers before they die are **luck, not robustness**, with $\operatorname{cond}(A^\mathsf{T}A)$ already $2\times10^{14}$. Finally, what fitting assumes: *linear* refers to the parameters and not to $x$; more columns always shrink the residual, so a smaller residual is never evidence of a better model; a middle outlier can produce a **larger** residual while doing **no** damage to the slope, because a point at $\bar x$ has no leverage; and **Week 0's Hilbert matrix was polynomial fitting all along**, since $H_{ij} = \int_0^1x^{i+j-2}dx$ is the Gram matrix of $1, x, x^2, \dots$ — which is why orthogonal polynomial families exist.
**Next —** Week 10 — symmetric matrices and the spectral theorem: $A^\mathsf{T}A$, the matrix at the centre of this week, is symmetric for every $A$, and symmetric matrices are never defective and always have orthonormal eigenvectors.
