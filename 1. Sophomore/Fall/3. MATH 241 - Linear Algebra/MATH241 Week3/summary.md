# MATH 241 · Week 3 · Summary

**Topic —** Linear independence, basis and dimension, and the four fundamental subspaces.
**Lectures —** L10 Linear Independence; L11 Basis and Dimension; L12 The Four Fundamental Subspaces.

**Takeaway —** Independence means the only combination giving zero is the trivial one — not "no vector is a multiple of another", which gets $(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)$ wrong — and stacking the vectors as columns turns it into $\mathbf{N}(X) = \{0\}$, so Week 0's algorithm already tests it. A basis is independent *and* spanning, and the two conditions buy the two halves of unique coordinates: spanning gives existence, independence gives uniqueness. **An independent list is never longer than a spanning list**, and two lines later every basis of a space has the same size — which is what makes *dimension* well defined and what retroactively licenses every count in Week 2, since the pivot count could otherwise have depended on how you eliminated. Applying Week 2's two constructions to $A^\mathsf{T}$ as well gives **four subspaces with dimensions $r$, $n-r$, $r$, $m-r$**, the pair in $\mathbb{R}^n$ adding to $n$ and the pair in $\mathbb{R}^m$ to $m$. The week's real fact is the mirror of Week 2's: **elimination combines rows, so it preserves the row space and moves the column space** — which is exactly why a row-space basis is read off $\operatorname{rref}$ while a column-space basis must be read back from $A$, and the asymmetry is a consequence rather than a convention. Both nonetheless have dimension $r$: **row rank $=$ column rank**, surprising because rows and columns are different vectors in different spaces and there are different numbers of them, and the elimination proof establishes it without explaining it. Finally the loop from Week 2 closes: the vector $(5,-2,1)$ used to write the equation of $\mathbf{C}(A)$ is a basis for $\mathbf{N}(A^\mathsf{T})$, **the left null space is the list of solvability conditions on $b$**, and $\dim\mathbf{N}(A^\mathsf{T}) = m - r$ says how many there are — one, here, matching the single zero row of $\operatorname{rref}$.
**Next —** Week 4 — linear transformations and their matrices: once coordinates are unique, a linear map *is* a matrix, and choosing the basis that makes it simple becomes the main tool of the rest of the course.
