# MATH 241 · Week 1 · Summary

**Topic —** Matrices as objects: multiplication derived rather than declared, the inverse and why not to compute one, transposes, and $A = LU$.
**Lectures —** L04 Matrix Multiplication, Four Ways; L05 The Inverse: Gauss–Jordan, and Why You Should Almost Never Compute One; L06 Transposes, Permutations, and $A = LU$.
**Work —** PS 1 (100, released Wednesday, due Friday of Week 2), Quiz 1 (Monday, covers Week 0, unmarked). **PS 0 due Friday.** Recitation 0 is sat Thursday and covers Week 0; Recitation 1 covers this week and is sat Thursday of Week 2.
**Takeaway —** Matrix multiplication is not a convention: requiring $(AB)x = A(Bx)$ forces both the formula and the shape rule, and the four readings of the product — entries, columns, rows, outer products — are the same number and four different ideas, of which the column reading is the default and the outer-product reading is Week 11. Multiplying costs $2n^3$ against elimination's $n^3/3$, a fixed factor of six, and the bracketing is worth more than the algorithm: $(AB)C$ against $A(BC)$ for a $1000\times2$ by $2\times1000$ by $1000\times1$ chain is $6{,}000{,}000$ flops against $8{,}000$, measured at **787× faster** with an 8 MB intermediate never formed. The inverse loses on all three counts — $2n^3$ against $n^3/3$ for the same $2n^2$ per solve, worse accuracy by an unpredictable factor of 2 to 91 on Hilbert systems, and a tridiagonal $K$ with 13 nonzeros whose inverse has 25, which at $n = 10^6$ is 8 terabytes against 32 megabytes of band. And elimination was factoring all along: $A = LU$ with $L$ **transcribed** from the multipliers rather than computed, $PA = LU$ when a row exchange is needed, and $P^{-1} = P^\mathsf{T}$ — the first orthogonal matrix, and the property the second half of the course is built on.
**Next —** Week 2 — vector spaces and subspaces: the set of reachable $b$ gets a name, the column space, and "every column of $AB$ is a combination of the columns of $A$" turns out to have been the point.
